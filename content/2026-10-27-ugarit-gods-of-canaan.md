## A plough, a tomb and a lost alphabet

For most of history, the gods of Canaan were known mainly through their enemies. The Hebrew Bible condemns Baal and Asherah, and a few late Greek writers summarised Phoenician myths, but no Canaanite had ever been heard speaking about their own gods.

That changed in 1928, when a peasant ploughing a field near the bay of Minet el-Beida, on the coast of northern Syria, accidentally opened an ancient tomb. The following year a French mission led by **Claude Schaeffer** began digging the large mound nearby, called Ras Shamra, about 10 kilometres north of Latakia. It turned out to be **Ugarit**, a wealthy port kingdom that flourished from roughly 1450 to 1200 BCE, trading with Egypt, the Hittites, Cyprus and the Aegean.

Among the first finds was a library of clay tablets in a script nobody had seen: cuneiform wedges pressed into clay like Babylonian writing, but with only about 30 signs. It was an alphabet, which scholars deciphered within a few years. The language, now called Ugaritic, is a close cousin of Hebrew and Phoenician. Its scribes also wrote Akkadian, the diplomatic language of the age, among seven scripts used in the city.

## A family of gods

The tablets reveal a crowded pantheon; one even lists Ugarit's gods beside their Babylonian equivalents. At its head sits **El**, the aged, bearded "father of mankind," often called "Bull El," who presides over the assembly of the gods. His consort is **Athirat** (the biblical Asherah), mother of the gods. The most energetic figure is **Baal**, whose name simply means "Lord"; the texts identify him with the storm god Hadad, whose home is Mount Zaphon (today's Jebel Aqra, on the Syrian-Turkish border). Around them move Baal's fierce sister **Anat**, the craftsman god **Kothar-wa-Khasis**, the sun goddess **Shapshu**, the sea god **Yam** and **Mot**, whose name means Death.

On the acropolis stood two temples, one for Baal and one usually linked to his father, Dagan. Nearby, the house of a high priest yielded many of the mythological tablets, alongside scribal school exercises.

## The Baal Cycle

The centrepiece is the **Baal Cycle**, six damaged tablets (catalogued as KTU 1.1 to 1.6) telling how the storm god won and kept his kingship. First Baal battles Yam, the Sea, who has been favoured by El; with two magic clubs made by Kothar, Baal strikes him down. Next he lobbies, through Anat and Athirat, for a palace of his own, and once it is built he thunders open its windows. Finally he challenges Mot, who swallows him. El mourns in dust and sackcloth, Anat searches the underworld, seizes Mot and cuts, burns and grinds him like grain, and at last Baal returns. The poem ends with a weary truce: Mot concedes that Baal is king.

What the story meant is debated. An older reading treated it as a seasonal myth, with Baal dying in the dry summer and rising with the autumn rains, one of the "dying and rising gods" of early twentieth-century comparative religion. Many specialists now doubt that neat scheme, and the historian of religion Ulf Oldenburg argued that Baal's death instead reflects a catastrophic drought when the rains fail. Aaron Tugendhaft reads it politically, as a meditation on the limits of kingship from a small kingdom dependent on the Hittite empire.

## The scribe who signed his work

Unusually, we know who wrote out these tablets. A colophon at the end of the cycle names "Ilimilku the Shubbanite," pupil of Attenu the diviner, chief of the priests, in the service of "Niqmaddu king of Ugarit." Ilimilku's hand appears on the Baal tablets and on two epics of human kings, Kirta and Aqhat.

Two questions remain open. Was Ilimilku a copyist of older oral poetry, an editor, or a genuine author? Scholars disagree. And which Niqmaddu did he serve? He was long dated to the mid-1300s, but a letter he wrote, found in 1994, led some scholars, including Nicolas Wyatt, to move him to the late 1200s, only a generation before the city's fall.

## Echoes in the Bible

Ugarit was not Israel, and its texts are centuries older than most of the Hebrew Bible, yet the overlap in language and imagery is striking. The Baal Cycle describes the storm god defeating Lotan, "the fleeing serpent," a creature of the sea with seven heads. Isaiah 27:1 promises that God will punish "leviathan the piercing serpent, even leviathan that crooked serpent" (KJV), using matching phrases; the Assyriologist W. G. Lambert went so far as to call it a direct quotation. Biblical poetry pictures God as a rider on the clouds, in combat with the Sea, presiding over a council of divine beings, and the Bible even names a place called Baal-zephon (Exodus 14:2), after Baal's mountain.

Early enthusiasts overstated the case. The more cautious consensus is that Israel's poets drew on a shared West Semitic religious vocabulary, and scholars such as Mark S. Smith argue that Israel's god absorbed titles once belonging to both El and Baal. How and when that happened remains one of the liveliest debates in the study of ancient religion.

## The end of the city

Ugarit was destroyed around 1185 BCE, during the wider collapse of the Late Bronze Age. A letter from its last king, Ammurapi, warns the king of Alashiya (probably Cyprus) that enemy ships have burned his towns while his troops are away in Hittite lands. The city was never rebuilt on the same scale. Fieldwork in the region, halted by Syria's civil war in 2011, resumed nearby in 2025.
