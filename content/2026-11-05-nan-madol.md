## A city that seems to float

Off the southeast coast of Pohnpei, a high volcanic island in the western Pacific, lies a cluster of ruins that looks almost impossible. Walls of dark, six-sided basalt "logs," stacked like timber, rise out of a shallow lagoon. They sit on artificial islets that people built on the coral reef, separated by tidal canals and shielded from the ocean by seawalls. This is **Nan Madol**, often loosely translated as "the spaces between," after those canals. Today it is part of the Federated States of Micronesia.

Counts of the islets vary with how you define one. UNESCO says "more than 100," the U.S. National Park Service says "over 90," and archaeologist Mark McCoy's team describes 98 islets spread across about 83 hectares of lagoon. Whatever the exact number, it is the largest archaeological site in Micronesia. For several centuries it was the seat of the **Saudeleur**, the first rulers remembered as controlling all of Pohnpei.

## Building with stone and reef

The builders worked with two materials. The first was columnar basalt, which forms naturally when lava cools and cracks into long prisms. They laid these columns in alternating directions, a "header-stretcher" pattern that UNESCO singles out as the site's signature technique. The second was coral. According to McCoy and his colleagues, people laid framing stones on the reef flat and filled the space between them with broken coral. They then raised double walls and packed the gap between those with more coral rubble. "We think of coral as precious," McCoy said, "but for the architects of Nan Madol it was a building material."

The scale is startling. The Park Service says the heaviest basalt pieces weigh around 100,000 pounds, though most are far smaller. Exactly how they were moved and set in place is still an open question; the Park Service calls the method unknown. Pohnpeian oral tradition gives its own answer: two sorcerer brothers, Olisihpa and Olosohpa, raised the city by magic. The site has also attracted modern "lost continent" theories. These have no archaeological support.

## The tomb of the first chief

The largest structure is **Nandauwas** (also spelled Nan Dowas). It is a walled royal mortuary about 80 by 60 metres, standing more than 8 metres tall, with outer walls up to about 3 metres thick, and a central burial vault roofed with basalt. Oral tradition says it was the tomb of the first Saudeleur.

For decades the site was dated mostly by radiocarbon, which can only narrow events to within about a century. In a 2016 paper in *Quaternary Research*, McCoy and colleagues used **uranium-thorium dating** on fresh branch coral that had been mined as building fill. If that coral was taken live from the reef, as they argue, the vault's enclosing wall was built between about 1172 and 1186 CE, and its rubble fill was added between 1197 and 1209. That puts the start of monument building at Nandauwas at roughly 1180–1200 CE. This is about a century earlier than the older estimate of about 1300, and earlier than similar monuments elsewhere in the Pacific. Using portable X-ray fluorescence (a handheld tool that reads a rock's chemistry), the team also matched some of the basalt to sources on the far side of Pohnpei. That suggests a ruler able to draw labour and stone from the whole island.

People were on Pohnpei much earlier, from about the first century CE. One coral sample from the fill of the islet itself dated to the 1st century CE. The authors note that this is close to the earliest secure evidence of people on the island, so the reef here may have been used long before the great walls went up.

## Lords of Deleur

According to the nomination dossier submitted to UNESCO, *Saudeleur* means "Lord of Deleur," the district around Nan Madol. Oral accounts disagree on how many there were, anywhere from eight to seventeen. Later generations of Pohnpeians counted back through the genealogies and placed the start of the dynasty around 1160. That is remarkably close to the coral dates, though genealogical reckoning is only approximate. The city was split into two halves. Archaeologists Joyce Bath and Stephen Athens, summarising the traditions, describe **Madol Powe** as the half reserved for priests and **Madol Pah** as the home of chiefs and their retainers. The Park Service places Nandauwas in Madol Powe. One islet, Idehd, was remembered for a sacred eel and a turtle offering.

The traditions remember the early Saudeleur as good rulers. The later ones are described as greedy and cruel. In the end, the last Saudeleur imprisoned the thunder god Nahn Sapwe. The god escaped east to Katau and fathered a son, **Isokelekel**, who sailed to Pohnpei with 333 companions and overthrew the dynasty. Isokelekel became the first **Nahnmwarki**, a chiefly title that still exists today. Kosraean traditions tell a version of the same hero, which is why many historians think Katau means the island of Kosrae. The Park Service gives the conquest the date 1628. That figure comes from interpreting the oral record, not from a written chronicle, so it should be treated as approximate.

## A debated transformation

How literally should the story be read? Bath and Athens's 1990 study tested the oral account against archaeology. They asked whether the shift from one island-wide Saudeleur state to several separate Nahnmwarki chiefdoms was a real political break or partly a later framing of history. The question has not been settled. It matters because Pohnpei is one of the clearest cases anywhere of a society becoming more centralized and then breaking apart again.

What seems clear is that Nan Madol lost its central role after the Saudeleur. The Park Service says it was abandoned in the 18th century. UNESCO says it was no longer lived in by the 1820s, though it kept its religious importance.

## A ruin in danger

In 2016 UNESCO added Nan Madol to the World Heritage List and, at the same time, to its List of World Heritage in Danger. Canals filling with silt let mangroves take root, and the roots push into the walls. Storm surges and fallen trees add to the damage. In 2025 the World Heritage Committee set out the conditions for taking it off the danger list. These focus on clearing five key islets, including Nandauwas, Idehd and Pahn Kedira, and on repairing their retaining walls. At its 2026 session the Committee welcomed progress, including more vegetation clearance and reopened canal routes, but kept Nan Madol on the danger list.
