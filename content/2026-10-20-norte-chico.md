## Pyramids in the desert

About 200 kilometres (120 miles) north of Lima, four short rivers cross one of the driest coasts on Earth. This stretch is called the **Norte Chico**, the "Little North," and its valleys are the **Huaura**, **Supe**, **Pativilca** and **Fortaleza**. Here, roughly between 3000 and 1800 BCE, people built at least thirty centres with stone platform mounds, plazas and houses, all before anyone in the region made pottery.

The best known is **Caral**, in the Supe Valley, about 23 kilometres inland. Its ruins were long thought to be fairly recent, until the Peruvian archaeologist **Ruth Shady Solís** began digging there in 1996 and found no potsherds, a strong hint of great age. In 2001 Shady and the American archaeologists **Jonathan Haas** and **Winifred Creamer** published radiocarbon dates in *Science*, from plant fibres tested at three laboratories. They placed Caral's monumental buildings and irrigated farming between about 2627 and 1977 BCE (calibrated). Shady's later project has dated the site more broadly to about 3000 to 1800 BCE.

That made Caral a busy centre while Egypt's pyramids were being built. UNESCO inscribed the **Sacred City of Caral-Supe** on the World Heritage List in 2009 and calls it the oldest centre of civilization in the Americas.

## A city of platforms and sunken plazas

Caral's central zone covers about 65 hectares and holds six large platform mounds, smaller mounds, sunken circular plazas and houses. The largest mound, the **Pirámide Mayor**, measured about 170 by 150 metres at its base and rose in terraces to a summit roughly 20 to 30 metres high. A broad staircase climbs to the top from a round, sunken plaza at its foot. UNESCO notes that such plazas and platform mounds went on to shape building along much of the Peruvian coast for centuries.

Workers filled loose-meshed reed bags, called **shicras**, with stones and stacked them behind retaining walls, so each terrace rose bag by bag. The reeds in these bags proved ideal for radiocarbon dating; when the first results came back in December 2000, Haas told *Smithsonian*, he "was virtually in hysterics for three days afterward."

Caral also had music. In a large sunken "amphitheatre" ringed by platform mounds, Shady's team found **32 flutes** made from pelican and condor bones, and in 2002 they found 37 cornets of deer and llama bone.

## Anchovies, cotton and the maize question

What fed this society is an old argument in Andean archaeology. In 1975 **Michael Moseley** proposed the **"maritime foundations"** hypothesis: the rich fishery of the Peruvian coast, rather than grain farming, gave people the surplus to build monuments.

The finds give both sides material. Caral's excavators found anchovies and sardines carried in from the coast, and cotton seeds, fibre or textiles in nearly every excavated building. Shady has suggested that inland farmers grew the cotton that coastal fishermen needed for nets and took dried fish and shellfish in exchange. In 2002 her team had found no maize at Caral, and she concluded that the city's power did not rest on stored grain. Maize has since turned up at Caral and nearby Miraya, in amounts not yet published.

Haas, Creamer and colleagues pushed further in a 2013 study of 13 sites in the Pativilca and Fortaleza valleys. Maize cobs and kernels were rare, but maize pollen turned up in nearly half of 126 soil samples, maize starch on 11 of 14 stone tools, and maize starch in 23 of 34 human coprolites (preserved feces). They argue that maize was a staple, not just a ceremonial food, while anchovies supplied protein. Even Moseley and his co-authors, in 2009, described a society "built on a combination of fishing and agriculture." The debate now is about the balance.

## Gods, strings and power

Norte Chico's beliefs are hard to read. In 2002 the Haas-Creamer project picked up a gourd-bowl fragment from the surface of a looted cemetery in the Pativilca Valley, incised and painted with a fanged figure with splayed feet holding a staff. The team identifies it as the earliest known image of the **Staff God**, a deity central to Chavín religion (about 1000 to 200 BCE) and later to the Wari and Tiwanaku empires. A small undecorated piece of the gourd was radiocarbon dated to about 2250 BCE. If they are right, Andean religion's best-known icon is about a thousand years older than once thought. But this is slight evidence from a disturbed context, and the link to a much later god is an interpretation, not proof.

A second contested find is a **quipu**, a knotted-string recording device of the kind the Inca later used for accounts. UNESCO's listing names a quipu found at Caral as evidence of the society's complexity. It is a single object, though, and until it is fully studied and published, quipu record-keeping this early remains a possibility, not an established fact.

The biggest argument is whether Norte Chico was a **state**. UNESCO describes Caral as a "fully developed socio-political state," and Shady has called it the mother city of the Inca civilization. Haas and Creamer disagree. They argue that there is "no scientific evidence that Caral was the capital of a 'Caral-Supe' state," that sites in the neighbouring valleys were just as large, and that the region was a decentralized network of peer centres. Whether Norte Chico counts as a "civilization" depends partly on how we define the word.

## Why the temples fell silent

By about 1800 BCE the great centres were winding down. According to a 2009 review, the last dates for temple sites in the Huaura and Supe valleys fall around 1875 BCE, and most of those in Pativilca and Fortaleza between about 1750 and 1650 BCE. Shady thinks Caral's people had time to bury some of their buildings carefully before leaving.

Why they left is still debated:

- **Earthquakes, floods and sand.** Daniel Sandweiss, Moseley, Shady and colleagues cite earthquake damage at the Pirámide Mayor and the coastal site of **Áspero**, flood layers they attribute to El Niño rains, and windblown sand over Caral's last occupation. They propose that a huge beach ridge, the Medio Mundo, sealed off the bays and fed sand sheets that buried fields and canals, but present this as a hypothesis still to be tested, since the ridge has not been dated.
- **Drought and resettlement.** In 2025 Shady's team argued that a severe drought about 4,200 years ago, possibly part of a wider dry spell some researchers link to crises in Mesopotamia and the Indus, drove people from Caral to new towns such as **Vichama** on the coast and **Peñico** upriver, dated to about 1800 to 1500 BCE. Vichama's temple friezes show emaciated bodies and a lightning-struck toad, which Shady reads as a memory of famine and hope for rain. A drought around 2200 BCE fits awkwardly with radiocarbon dates showing Caral still in use after 2000 BCE, so the sequence is still being worked out.

If Shady is right, Caral's people did not vanish but moved and adapted. Their sunken plazas and platform mounds reappeared along the coast, and the Staff God, if that gourd really shows him, outlived them by nearly three thousand years.
