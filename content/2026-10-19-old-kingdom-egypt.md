## A kingdom built in stone

Egyptologists call the period of the Third to Sixth Dynasties the **Old Kingdom**. In the widely used chronology of Ian Shaw's *Oxford History of Ancient Egypt* it runs from about 2686 to 2181 BCE, though other schemes move those dates by decades. From **Memphis**, near modern Cairo, its kings poured harvests and manpower into one kind of monument: the royal tomb.

To quarry, move and set millions of blocks, a state needed scribes, granaries, boats, census-takers and a steady supply of bread and beer. The pyramids are a record of one of the world's first large territorial states learning to organise itself.

## From step to true pyramid

The story begins with King **Djoser** (known in his own time as Netjerikhet), who reigned in roughly 2667 to 2648 BCE. His tomb at Saqqara started as a conventional flat-topped *mastaba*, then grew into six stacked stages about 62 metres high. It is usually described as Egypt's oldest monumental stone building. Most Egyptologists credit its design to Djoser's official **Imhotep**, though that rests partly on much later tradition.

![A six-stepped stone pyramid rising from a sandy plain under a blue sky, with a line of visitors on a path to the right](images/old-kingdom-djoser-step-pyramid.jpg)
*The Step Pyramid of Djoser at Saqqara, built in about the 27th century BCE. Photo: Vyacheslav Argenberg, [CC BY 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/), via [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Saqqara,_Pyramid_of_Djoser,_Ancient_Egypt.jpg).*

The next leap came under **Sneferu** of the Fourth Dynasty (about 2613 to 2589 BCE), who built three huge pyramids. At **Meidum** a step pyramid was converted into a smooth-sided one. At **Dahshur** the **Bent Pyramid** was planned as a true pyramid but changes angle partway up, apparently after structural problems. His **Red Pyramid**, also at Dahshur, is generally regarded as the first successfully completed true pyramid.

## Khufu's horizon and the people who built it

Sneferu's son **Khufu** (about 2589 to 2566 BCE) built the **Great Pyramid** at Giza, originally about 146.5 metres tall and for thousands of years the tallest human-made structure on Earth. His successors **Khafre**, usually credited with the Great Sphinx, and **Menkaure** added the plateau's other two great pyramids.

![The Great Pyramid of Giza seen from the desert, with horsemen and fallen stone blocks in the foreground](images/old-kingdom-great-pyramid-khufu.jpg)
*The Great Pyramid of Khufu at Giza, which the Egyptians called Akhet-Khufu, "Horizon of Khufu." Photo: kallerna, [CC BY-SA 3.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/3.0/), via [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Great_Pyramid_of_Giza.jpg).*

Two discoveries have changed how we picture the builders. In 2013 a team led by **Pierre Tallet** found papyri at **Wadi al-Jarf**, an ancient harbour on the Red Sea, that are the oldest inscribed papyri yet known. The best preserved is the logbook of an inspector named **Merer**, whose crew of roughly 40 boatmen spent several months, between about July and November, ferrying limestone from the **Tura** quarries across the Nile floodplain to "Akhet-Khufu," making two or three round trips every ten days, near the very end of Khufu's reign. The logbook does not say where the blocks went. Tallet thinks they were for the pyramid's now-vanished outer casing; Mark Lehner suggests the casing was finished by then and the stone may have roofed the pits for Khufu's funerary boats.

At Giza, Lehner's team at Ancient Egypt Research Associates has since 1988 excavated **Heit el-Ghurab**, a town of more than seven hectares south of the Sphinx with probable barracks, bakeries, granaries and administrators' houses. Nearby, **Zahi Hawass** excavated tombs of workers and overseers whose skeletons show heavy labour but also healed fractures, a sign of medical care. The old image of slave armies, which owes much to Greek writers such as Herodotus and to Hollywood, does not fit this evidence. The builders seem to have been a mix of skilled permanent craftsmen and rotating crews, though how freely the conscripted labourers served is still debated.

## Sun temples and spells for the afterlife

After Khufu, pyramids grew smaller. Menkaure's is far smaller, but its temples are relatively larger, which some scholars read as a shift from the tomb toward the king's ongoing cult. Fifth Dynasty kings also built **sun temples** to the god Ra near Abusir, of which only two, Userkaf's and Niuserre's, have been located on the ground.

At the very end of that dynasty, King **Unas** (about 2375 to 2345 BCE) had the walls of his burial chamber at Saqqara carved with the **Pyramid Texts**, 283 spells to help the dead king rise to the sky and join Ra. They are among the world's oldest religious texts, and Sixth Dynasty kings and queens copied and expanded them.

## Power drifts to the provinces

By the Sixth Dynasty, regional governors called **nomarchs** were building rich tombs far from Memphis. One official, **Harkhuf** of Aswan, carved on his tomb a letter from the young King **Pepy II**, sent in the king's second year, about a "dancing dwarf" Harkhuf was bringing back from the southern land of Yam. In James Henry Breasted's 1906 translation, the king orders his men to "inspect ten times a night" and says, "My majesty desires to see this dwarf more than the gifts of Sinai and of Punt."

![Timeline of Egypt's Old Kingdom from about 2700 to 2150 BCE](images/old-kingdom-timeline.svg)
*An approximate timeline of the Old Kingdom. Reign dates follow the consensus chronology and are debated, especially for the late Sixth Dynasty. Diagram: Obsidian Archive.*

Pepy II's reign is a puzzle in itself. Later sources give him 90 or 94 years, but because Old Kingdom scribes counted years by cattle censuses that were sometimes held only every other year, the surviving evidence allows anything from just over 30 to more than 60 years. A 2023 radiocarbon study of the mummy of **Djau**, one of his senior officials, suggests the Old Kingdom had ended by about 2200 BCE.

Why it ended is still argued. Rising provincial power and shrinking royal resources played a part, and many researchers also point to a drier climate around 4,200 years ago and lower Nile floods; Nadine Moeller, reviewing the evidence in 2005, found signs of gradual drying but no proof of a sudden catastrophe. Most scholars now see the First Intermediate Period that followed less as a collapse of Egyptian civilisation than as a time when power was shared out among the provinces.
