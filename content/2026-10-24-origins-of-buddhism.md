## A pillar that says "here"

In 1896 a buried sandstone pillar came to light at Rummindei, in the Terai lowlands of southern Nepal. Its short inscription, in the Brahmi script, reads in the classic 1925 translation by the epigraphist E. Hultzsch:

"When King Devanampriya Priyadarsin had been anointed twenty years, he came himself and worshipped (this spot) because the Buddha Shakyamuni was born here."

The king is **Ashoka**, the Mauryan emperor of India. The inscription adds that he set up a stone pillar "(in order to show) that the Blessed One was born here," and made the village of Lummini (Lumbini) free of taxes, paying only an eighth share of the produce. Ashoka's reign can be pinned down because another of his edicts names five Hellenistic kings; on that basis his consecration is usually placed around 268 BCE, and UNESCO dates the Lumbini visit to 249 BCE.

That makes the pillar our earliest firm, datable statement about the Buddha, and it already treats him as a revered figure whose birthplace deserved a royal pilgrimage. Everything before it depends on texts written down much later, or on archaeology.

## When did the Buddha live?

Nobody knows for sure. The Theravada tradition of Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia places the Buddha's death (his *parinirvana*) in 544 or 543 BCE, the starting point of the Buddhist Era still used today; UNESCO's description of Lumbini follows this tradition in giving his birth as 623 BCE. Few modern historians accept it, because the same chronicles put Ashoka's consecration around 326 BCE, roughly sixty years too early.

From the mid-1800s, Western scholars mostly settled on a "corrected" date of about 560 to 480 BCE. That consensus broke in 1988, at a symposium on the Buddha's date organised by **Heinz Bechert** in Göttingen. Most participants placed his death within a few decades either side of 400 BCE: Richard Gombrich argued for about 404 BCE, K. R. Norman for 410 to 390, and others for dates as late as 368. As the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy puts it, there is no conclusive evidence for any specific date.

Archaeology has re-entered the argument. Between 2011 and 2013 a team led by **Robin Coningham** and **Kosh Prasad Acharya** dug beneath the Maya Devi Temple at Lumbini, which stands over Ashoka's brick temple. They found earlier brick pavements and, below them, a line of postholes from a timber structure. Radiocarbon dates from two posthole fills came out at about 800 to 550 BCE, and the team interpreted the space as the earliest known Buddhist shrine, from around the sixth century BCE. That fits the older dating better than the "short" one, but it rests on interpretation: a sacred space at Lumbini could pre-date Buddhist use of it, and the debate over the Buddha's dates remains open.

## A world of wanderers

Whenever he lived, the Buddha belonged to a crowded religious scene. The eastern Ganges plain, around the kingdoms of Magadha and Kosala, was growing cities, trade and states. It was also full of *shramanas*, wandering renouncers who left household life to seek release from the cycle of rebirth. One of them was **Mahavira**, the great teacher of the Jains, whom both traditions remember as active in the same region.

How these movements related to the Vedic religion of the Brahmin priests is a live debate. The Indologist **Johannes Bronkhorst** argues that "Greater Magadha" had its own non-Vedic culture, in which ideas of rebirth and karmic retribution took shape. Critics reply that Vedic and shramana traditions cannot be separated so neatly.

## The middle way

Buddhist texts say that after his awakening at Bodh Gaya the Buddha taught his first sermon to five former companions in the deer park at Isipatana, now **Sarnath**, near Varanasi. In the Pali version, the *Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta*, he rejects two extremes, indulgence in sensual pleasure and "self-affliction," in favour of "the middle way," the Noble Eightfold Path, and sets out the Four Noble Truths about suffering (*dukkha*, translated by Thanissaro Bhikkhu as "stress"), its origin in craving, its cessation and the path to that cessation.

Even this famous text has its doubters: some scholars have argued that the phrase "noble truth," or even the four truths themselves, were added later, though others reject the linguistic case for this. Sarnath itself is solid archaeology. The Archaeological Survey of India reports monasteries, stupas and inscriptions there from the third century BCE to the twelfth century CE, including a broken Ashokan pillar whose four-lion capital is now India's national emblem.

## Remembered, not written

For centuries the teachings were memorised and chanted, not written. Sri Lanka's oldest chronicle, the *Dipavamsa*, composed not long after 350 CE, says that monks first wrote the scriptures in books during the reign of King Vattagamani in the first century BCE, so "that the Religion might endure for a long time." The oldest surviving Buddhist manuscripts are birch-bark scrolls from the Gandhara region of today's Pakistan and Afghanistan, in the Gandhari language and Kharoshthi script. The University of Washington's Early Buddhist Manuscripts Project dates them from the first century BCE to the third century CE, and they include versions of texts also known in Pali, such as the "Rhinoceros Sutra."

So the words attributed to the Buddha passed through generations of memory, editing and rivalry between schools. Scholars disagree about how much of the earliest layer goes back to him personally.

## Relics and remembrance

Early Buddhists also honoured the Buddha's remains. In 1898 the English estate manager **William Claxton Peppé** opened a stupa at Piprahwa in northern India, close to the Nepal border. Inside were reliquaries, bone fragments and nearly 1,800 pearls, gems and gold pieces, with a vase whose short inscription refers to relics of the Buddha and the Shakyas, his clan. Scholars have argued since 1898 about its exact wording and date. The bones went to the King of Siam; in 2025 Sotheby's postponed an auction of the gems kept by the Peppé family after India's government objected, and they were returned to India that July.
