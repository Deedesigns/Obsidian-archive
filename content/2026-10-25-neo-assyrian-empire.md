## A city, a god and a land

On the Tigris in northern Iraq, at the line where rain-fed farming gives way to irrigation, stands the ancient city of **Ashur**. As UNESCO notes, the city, its god and the whole land shared one name. Ashur was where Assyrian kings were crowned and buried, and from roughly the 14th to the 9th century BCE it was the kingdom's capital.

Historians call the last and greatest phase the **Neo-Assyrian Empire**, conventionally dated from about 912 to 609 BCE. A 2019 study in *Science Advances* calls it "the largest and most powerful empire of its time." At its height in the 7th century BCE, Assyrian governors and vassals controlled lands from western Iran to the Mediterranean, and for a while even Egypt.

## Palaces built to impress

In about 879 BCE King **Ashurnasirpal II** finished a new capital at **Kalhu**, modern Nimrud. His so-called Banquet Stela, found by British archaeologists in 1951, claims he celebrated with a ten-day feast for 69,574 guests, washed down with 10,000 jugs of beer and 10,000 skins of wine, before sending everyone home "in peace and joy." Royal numbers deserve some caution, but the boast tells us what kings wanted remembered.

Huge stone **lamassu**, guardian spirits with human heads, wings and the bodies of bulls or lions, flanked the palace doorways. Their carvers gave them five legs, so they look like they're standing when seen from the front and striding when seen from the side. Inside, wall panels showed the king hunting, worshipping and conquering.

## Empire by deportation

Assyria's real growth came under **Tiglath-pileser III** (745 to 727 BCE), who turned conquered kingdoms into provinces and made mass deportation a routine policy. Whole communities were uprooted and resettled far away, both to break resistance and to put people to work where the state needed them. In 1979 the historian **Bustenay Oded** estimated from Assyrian records that about 4.4 million people were moved over roughly two and a half centuries. The figure is often rounded to 4.5 million, but it rests on royal inscriptions that may inflate their totals. The best-known victims are the people of Israel, deported after Samaria fell in about 722 BCE.

Later kings kept building. **Sargon II** began a brand-new capital, **Dur-Sharrukin** (Khorsabad), in about 717 BCE. His son **Sennacherib** (704 to 681 BCE) moved the court to **Nineveh** and gave it canals, aqueducts and a palace he called "without rival."

## Lachish: a siege in stone

In 701 BCE Sennacherib marched against the kingdom of Judah and besieged its second city, **Lachish**. Back in Nineveh he had the campaign carved around one room of his palace, the most detailed siege scene any Assyrian king commissioned. Soldiers storm the walls, families leave with their belongings, and, according to the British Museum's description of one panel, officials blamed for the rebellion are flayed alive. The king watches from his throne, though in antiquity someone hacked away his face.

![A carved stone frieze with two rows of soldiers carrying spears and round shields marching above a row of horses](images/assyria-lachish-relief.jpg)

Photo: Mike Peel, [CC BY-SA 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/), via [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Lachish_Relief,_British_Museum_1.jpg).*

These panels were not neutral reporting. They decorated the king's own halls, and scholars usually read them as a message to everyone who walked past. The British Museum points out that the campaign had a limit too. Sennacherib claimed victory, but he never captured Jerusalem.

Nineveh also sits at the centre of a lively modern debate. Assyriologist **Stephanie Dalley** has argued that the famous Hanging Gardens of Babylon were really Sennacherib's gardens at Nineveh, fed by his aqueducts and muddled by later Greek writers. Critics such as **Timothy Potts** answer that the Greek sources consistently put the gardens in Babylon. Treat the theory as intriguing but unproven.

## The library of Ashurbanipal

Sennacherib's grandson **Ashurbanipal** (669 to about 631 BCE) prided himself on being able to read. His scholars gathered omens, medical texts, hymns and literature at Nineveh. Excavations from 1850 onward, first by **Austen Henry Layard** and then his Iraqi-born assistant **Hormuzd Rassam**, recovered what the British Museum now counts as more than 30,000 tablets and fragments. When Nineveh burned, the fire baked much of the clay harder and helped preserve it.

![A broken, rectangular clay tablet covered in rows of small wedge-shaped cuneiform signs, displayed upright in a museum case](images/assyria-flood-tablet.jpg)
*The "Flood Tablet," Tablet 11 of the Epic of Gilgamesh from Ashurbanipal's library, 7th century BCE. British Museum, K.3375. Photo: Mike Peel, [CC BY-SA 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/), via [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:British_Museum_Flood_Tablet_1.jpg).*

In 1872 a self-taught former banknote engraver, **George Smith**, was sorting these fragments when, in his words, his eye "caught the statement that the ship rested on the mountains of Nizir." He had found a Mesopotamian flood story strikingly like Noah's, and his lecture that December caused a sensation.

## A sudden end

The fall came fast. Babylon broke away under **Nabopolassar**, and the Medes of Iran joined him. According to the Babylonian chronicle now called the *Fall of Nineveh Chronicle*, the Medes sacked Ashur in 614 BCE, and the allies took Nineveh in 612 BCE after a siege of about three months. A last king, **Ashur-uballit II**, held out at Harran with Egyptian help, but after 609 BCE Assyria disappears from the record.

Why did such a powerful state collapse so completely? Older explanations point to civil war, overstretch and military defeat. The 2019 cave study adds a climate factor: a cave record from northern Iraq suggests that Assyria's expansion coincided with two unusually wet centuries, and that decades-long droughts began in the 7th century BCE. Its authors argue the droughts weakened Assyria's farming base and *contributed* to the collapse, alongside the political causes. Climate links like this are hard to prove, so treat drought as a possible contributor, not a settled cause.

Assyria's ruins have suffered again in our own time. In March 2015, UNESCO condemned the bulldozing of Nimrud by Islamic State militants as a war crime. The lamassu and tablets in museums abroad are now among the best-preserved witnesses to the empire.
