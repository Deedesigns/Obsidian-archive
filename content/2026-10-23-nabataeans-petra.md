## Raiders at "the rock"

The **Nabataeans** enter written history in a war story. The Greek historian **Diodorus Siculus**, writing in the first century BCE, says that in about 312 or 311 BCE Antigonus the One-Eyed, one of Alexander the Great's successors, sent his officer **Athenaeus** with 4,600 men to raid "the Arabs who are called Nabataeans."

Diodorus describes them as desert herders who refused to plant grain, drink wine or build houses, so that no powerful neighbour could bully them. Although "not much more than ten thousand in number," they were the richest of the Arab tribes, because many carried **frankincense, myrrh** and spices from southern Arabia to the sea. In danger they vanished into waterless country, where they had hidden plaster-lined cisterns marked with signs only they could read.

Athenaeus struck at night at "a certain rock" where the Nabataeans had left their families and goods while the men were at a market. He seized incense, myrrh and about 500 talents of silver, but the Nabataeans caught his sleeping camp and, says Diodorus, killed all the infantry; about fifty horsemen escaped. They then complained to Antigonus in a letter "in Syrian characters," probably Aramaic script.

Was the rock Petra? Diodorus's Loeb editor thought it "may be the later Petra," but the archaeologist **Laurent Tholbecq**, in a 2025 survey, warns that Diodorus "might actually not be referring to Petra at all." His portrait of tent-dwelling purists is also an outsider's view, written more than two centuries later.

## From tents to a royal capital

Deep trenches in downtown Petra, in southern Jordan, have reached remains radiocarbon dated to the late fifth or early fourth century BCE, older than any written mention of the city. Tholbecq thinks the first residents probably lived in tents on terraces below the outcrop of el-Habis, and that the first large buildings rose only in the late third and second centuries BCE.

By then the Nabataeans had kings: a poem by Posidippus of Pella mentions one in the second quarter of the third century BCE. Petra appears as a seat of Nabataean power by 168 BCE, in the second book of Maccabees' story of the deposed Jewish high priest Jason, and an inscription of 129 BCE records an embassy from the Greek city of Priene to a king at Petra.

Their wealth came from trade. UNESCO describes Petra as a caravan centre for "the incense of Arabia, the silks of China and the spices of India." The Greek geographer **Strabo**, writing around the turn of the era, says that camel traders travelled "in such numbers of men and camels that they differ in no respect from an army," and that loads of aromatics went from the Red Sea port of Leuke Kome to Petra and on to the Mediterranean coast near Egypt. The kingdom's reach extended west into the Negev and south into northwest Arabia, where the Nabataean city of **Hegra** (Mada'in Salih in modern Saudi Arabia) has its own field of carved tombs.

## A city carved and watered

Petra peaked under **Obodas** (r. 30–9 BCE; numbered II or III, depending on the scholar) and **Aretas IV** (r. 9 BCE–40 CE). The city gained a colonnaded street, the great temple now called **Qasr al-Bint**, a probable palace with a garden and monumental pool, and many of its roughly **620 rock-cut tomb façades**, which mix local "crow-step" crowns with Greek columns and Egyptian cornices. UNESCO calls this fusion "a unique artistic achievement."

The 39.5-metre **Khazneh** or "Treasury" is named for a Bedouin legend of a pharaoh's gold hidden in its stone urn, which is pocked with bullet marks. It is actually a royal tomb. In 2003 Jordanian archaeologists found older tombs beneath it, which the Treasury's carving partly cut away; Tholbecq says this evidence securely dates it to the reign of Obodas or Aretas IV. He tentatively suggests it was planned after the death of Aretas's first wife, Huldu, around 16 to 19 CE. In 2024 a Jordanian, American and Scottish team found another tomb beneath it holding 12 skeletons, its walls dated by sediment analysis to between the mid-first century BCE and the early second century CE.

None of this works without water. Strabo heard that desert Petra had "springs in abundance, both for domestic purposes and for watering gardens," thanks to a system UNESCO counts among the site's outstanding features: channels, tunnels, diversion dams, cisterns and reservoirs. At the entrance to the **Siq**, the narrow gorge into the city, the Nabataeans dammed the stream and cut an 88-metre tunnel to send flash floods around the gorge. When maintenance stopped, the floods returned; today's dam was built over the larger Nabataean one in 1964, after a tragic flood.

## Gods, kings and a puzzle in Strabo

Strabo gives the liveliest, and strangest, description of Nabataean society. His friend Athenodorus had visited Petra and admired it: foreigners there often sued each other, but locals never did. The king served at his own banquets and often rendered "an account of his kingship in the popular assembly," and anyone whose possessions shrank was fined. Strabo adds that the Nabataeans "bury even their kings beside dung-heaps" and worshipped the sun on rooftop altars.

The burial claim is hard to square with hundreds of grand tombs, often fronted by courtyards with gardens, cisterns and dining rooms for funerary banquets. Strabo was writing secondhand, and Tholbecq notes that Athenodorus was a tutor and likely envoy of Augustus, so his account may be politically coloured. Strabo's story of a Roman expedition into Arabia in the 20s BCE, supposedly sabotaged by the king's chief minister **Syllaeus**, is likewise told only from the Roman side.

The Nabataeans' own voice is thin: Petra has yielded fewer than a thousand Nabataean inscriptions, about 90 percent of them simple signatures. Their chief god, **Dushara**, seems to be named after the local Shara mountains, and worship took place in temples and at "high places" on the peaks. Much else about their beliefs is interpretation.

## Rome, earthquakes and a long afterlife

In 106 CE, after the death of the last king, Rabbel II, the emperor **Trajan** made the kingdom the Roman province of **Arabia**. Most scholars have called the takeover peaceful; Trajan's coins say *Arabia adquisita* ("acquired"), not *capta* ("captured"). Others, such as Fahad al-Otaibi, cite catapult balls at Petra and desert graffiti about war with Rome as signs of resistance. The evidence, Tholbecq writes, is "frustratingly equivocable."

Petra lived on, with Roman baths and the title *Hadriane Petra*. What hurt it most were earthquakes in 363 and 418 CE, and by Byzantine times much of its population had apparently moved to the highlands. The Urn Tomb became a church in 446, and in 1993 excavators of another church found about 140 carbonized papyrus rolls, a sixth-century family archive in Greek. The Swiss traveller **Johann Ludwig Burckhardt** reached the ruins in 1812, using a visit to the pilgrimage shrine of Aaron on nearby Jebel Harun as his pretext.

The Nabataeans left one more legacy: their form of the Aramaic alphabet evolved, through transitional "Nabataeo-Arabic" letters, into the **Arabic script**. A tomb inscription for a woman named Raqush at Hegra, dated to 267 CE and written mostly in Arabic in Nabataean letters, is the earliest known dated text of its kind.
