## A prophet without a date

Zoroastrianism takes its name from **Zarathustra**, known to the Greeks as Zoroaster, a priest and poet of the ancient Iranian peoples. Almost everything about his life is uncertain. Later Zoroastrian tradition placed him "258 years before Alexander," in the sixth century BCE, but many scholars think that figure was a later calculation. Because the language of his hymns is very close to the Sanskrit of the oldest Indian *Rigveda*, most specialists now place him much earlier, somewhere between roughly 1500 and 1000 BCE, with about 1000 BCE a common estimate. A minority still argue for the later date, and the question remains open.

His homeland is just as hard to pin down. The places named in the oldest texts point to eastern Iran and Central Asia rather than western Persia, where the religion later became powerful. He seems to have lived among cattle-herding communities that had no cities and no writing.

## The Gathas and the Wise Lord

Zarathustra's own words probably survive in the **Gathas**, seventeen hymns in an archaic language called Old Avestan. They are difficult poems, and translations differ widely. Their core message is clear enough: one supreme god, **Ahura Mazda** ("Wise Lord"), stands for *asha*, truth and right order, against *druj*, the lie or deception. Every person must choose between them, in thought, word and deed.

The hymns also speak of divine qualities or helpers, later known as the **Amesha Spentas** ("Beneficent Immortals"), and of a judgement after death at the "Bridge of the Separator." Later texts describe that bridge as wide for the righteous and narrow as a blade for the wicked, and they look ahead to a final renewal of the world, when evil would be defeated for good. Whether early Zoroastrianism was strictly monotheistic or a reformed form of older Iranian religion is a long-running debate; scholars such as Almut Hintze have argued for a distinctive "Zoroastrian way" of monotheism that still honours lesser divine beings.

## Under the Persian kings

When the **Achaemenid** Persian Empire rose in the sixth century BCE, its kings invoked Ahura Mazda. Darius I (ruled about 522–486 BCE) declared in his inscriptions that he ruled "by the favour of Ahuramazda." Yet the inscriptions never mention Zarathustra, and historians still debate whether the Achaemenids were Zoroastrians in a full sense or followers of a broader Iranian religion that honoured the same god. Greek writers such as Herodotus described Persian priests called **magi** and customs such as sacrifice in the open air, care not to pollute rivers, and leaving the dead to be torn by birds or dogs before burial.

The winged figure carved at Persepolis and on royal tombs is now known as the **faravahar** and has become the best-known Zoroastrian symbol. Its original meaning is uncertain: it has been read as Ahura Mazda, as the king's divine glory, or as a *fravashi* (guardian spirit), and it owes much to older Assyrian winged-disk images.

## Fire, priests and a written scripture

After Alexander the Great conquered Persia in the 330s BCE, the faith continued under the Parthians. It reached its height of official power under the **Sasanian** dynasty (224–651 CE), whose founder, Ardashir I, had himself depicted receiving the ring of kingship from Ahura Mazda. Under the Sasanians a priestly hierarchy grew strong; inscriptions of the third-century high priest **Kartir** boast of founding fire temples and of persecuting Jews, Christians, Buddhists, Manichaeans and others.

For centuries the sacred texts had been passed down by memory. Only around the fifth or sixth century CE did priests invent a special **Avestan alphabet** and write the scriptures down as the Sasanian Avesta. Much of that collection was later lost; the oldest surviving manuscript of an Avestan text dates to 1323 CE. Fire, seen as a pure sign of Ahura Mazda's truth rather than a god to be worshipped, became the centre of temple ritual.

## Conquest, migration and survival

The Arab conquest of Iran in the seventh century ended Zoroastrianism's role as a state religion. Conversion to Islam was gradual, taking several centuries, and Zoroastrian priests went on writing important works in Middle Persian, such as the *Denkard* and *Bundahishn*, in the ninth and tenth centuries. Some communities moved to western India, where their descendants became the **Parsis**; the traditional story of their arrival, the *Qissa-i Sanjan*, is hard to date, and estimates range from the eighth to the tenth century.

![A Zoroastrian fire temple with columned portico behind a circular pool in Yazd](images/yazd-atash-behram-fire-temple.jpg)

Today perhaps around 100,000 to 200,000 Zoroastrians live worldwide, mainly in India, Iran and diaspora communities. Some scholars argue that Zoroastrian ideas about angels, a final judgement and resurrection influenced Judaism, Christianity and Islam, especially during and after the Persian period. Others think those links are overstated, and how much was borrowed, and in which direction, is still argued over.
