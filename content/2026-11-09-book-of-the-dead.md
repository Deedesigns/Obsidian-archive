## Not a bible, and not one book

The name is modern. In 1842 the German Egyptologist Karl Richard Lepsius published a Ptolemaic-era scroll in Turin and called it the *Todtenbuch*, "Book of the Dead." He also gave its sections the numbers scholars still use. The Egyptians called these texts something closer to *Spells of Coming Forth by Day* (*rw nw prt m hrw*): words that would let the dead person leave the tomb by daylight and move freely in the next world.

Early European visitors who found these scrolls in tombs often assumed they were an Egyptian scripture, a kind of Bible. They were not. There was no fixed canon. Scholars now count roughly 192 spells, but no single manuscript contains them all. Each owner, or the workshop that sold them a scroll, picked a selection, and the illustrations (called vignettes) vary just as much. Many scrolls were prefabricated with blank spaces where a buyer's name could be added later; in the famous Papyrus of Ani, the owner's name appears in a different hand and is sometimes misspelled or missing.

## From pyramid walls to papyrus

The spells grew out of older funerary writing. The earliest are the **Pyramid Texts**, carved inside the pyramid of King Unas at Saqqara at the end of the Fifth Dynasty, roughly 2350 BCE. They were reserved at first for kings (and from the Sixth Dynasty, queens), and they imagine the dead ruler rising to the sky to join the sun god Ra.

By the Middle Kingdom (very roughly 2000–1700 BCE), new **Coffin Texts** were painted inside the coffins of officials and other wealthy people. Egyptologists often call this a "democratization of the afterlife," although the phrase is debated, since only a well-off minority could pay for such burials. The first spells that belong to the Book of the Dead proper appear around 1650–1600 BCE on the coffin of a Theban queen named Mentuhotep, mixed with older texts. Some new spells even claimed ancient pedigrees. The instructions for Spell 30B say it was found by Prince Hordjedef in the reign of Menkaure, centuries before any copy we actually have, which is a claim of prestige rather than a reliable date.

In the New Kingdom, from about 1550 BCE, the collection moved onto papyrus rolls. A good scroll was expensive. One ancient source puts the price at a deben of silver, which may have been around half a laborer's yearly pay, so owners were mostly scribes, priests, officials and their families.

## The weighing of the heart

The most famous scene, linked to **Spell 125**, is first attested around the reigns of Hatshepsut and Thutmose III, roughly 1475 BCE. The dead person is led into a hall of judgment. There they address forty-two divine assessors by name and declare, one by one, the wrongs they did not commit, a text modern scholars call the "Negative Confession." Then the heart, which Egyptians saw as the seat of thought, memory and character, is weighed on a balance against the feather of Maat, goddess of truth and right order.

![Papyrus vignette of Anubis weighing a heart against a feather, with Thoth recording and Ammit waiting](images/book-of-dead-hunefer-judgement.jpg)
*The judgment scene from the Book of the Dead of the royal scribe Hunefer, about 1275 BCE (British Museum EA 9901): Anubis leads Hunefer in and tends the scales, Ammit crouches beside them, Thoth records, and Horus presents Hunefer to the enthroned Osiris. Image: public domain, via [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_judgement_of_the_dead_in_the_presence_of_Osiris.jpg).*

Anubis steadies the scales and ibis-headed Thoth writes down the result. Beside the balance crouches Ammit, the Devourer, described on Hunefer's papyrus as crocodile in front, lion in the middle and hippopotamus behind. A balanced heart meant the dead person was declared "true of voice" and could go on to Osiris. A failed heart would be eaten, ending that person's existence.

## Morality or magic?

Here the scholarly argument gets interesting. Spell 30B is addressed to the heart itself and asks it not to stand up as a witness against its owner during the weighing. As John Taylor of the British Museum has pointed out, that sounds less like a test of pure virtue and more like a safeguard against inconvenient truths. Ogden Goelet stresses the moral side: without a good life there was no hope of a good afterlife. Geraldine Pinch argues that the Negative Confession works much like the spells against demons, so success depended largely on knowing the judges' true names. Most Egyptologists would say the Egyptians did not separate religion and magic the way we do. Spoken and written words (*heka*, magical power) were believed to make things happen.

That is why spells were also carved on objects. **Heart scarabs**, stone amulets placed over the mummy's chest, often carried a version of Spell 30B.

## Gates, monsters and the Field of Reeds

The rest of the spells read like a survival kit. They protected the body, made sure the dead person remembered their own name (Spell 25), fed the *ka* or life force, and kept the *ba*, the human-headed bird-soul, free to fly in and out of the tomb. Others named the knife-wielding guardians of the gates of the underworld so they could be passed safely, or helped the dead join Ra's sun boat. One goal was the **Field of Reeds**, an idealized Egypt of canals and harvests where work still had to be done. Small figurines called shabtis were buried to do that work in the owner's place.

## A long afterlife of its own

After about 1070 BCE, cheaper copies written in hieratic, a cursive script, became common, and many belonged to women. Around the seventh century BCE, scribes of the Twenty-fifth and Twenty-sixth Dynasties standardized the order of the spells, producing what is called the Saite recension. Copies continued into the Ptolemaic period. In 2022 an Egyptian mission led by Mostafa Waziri found a 16-meter Ptolemaic-era hieratic scroll inside the coffin of a man named Ahmose at Saqqara; reports give it about 113 spells. The last copies were made in roughly the first century BCE, as newer texts such as the Book of Breathing took over.

Today the University of Bonn's Book of the Dead Project has documented most of the surviving copies and fragments. Its archive is a reminder that there was never one "Book of the Dead," only thousands of personal ones.
