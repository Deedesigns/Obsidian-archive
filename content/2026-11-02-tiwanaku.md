## A city on the roof of the Andes

Tiwanaku lies in a valley near the southern shore of Lake Titicaca, about 15 kilometres from the water. It sits at roughly 3,850 metres above sea level in Bolivia's La Paz department. For several centuries in the first millennium CE it was the centre of one of the most influential societies in the ancient Andes. UNESCO added the site to its World Heritage List in 2000.

The Titicaca basin had a long ceremonial tradition before Tiwanaku. The archaeologist **Alexei Vranich** notes that the region's typical form of public architecture, the sunken court, goes back to about 1800 BCE. Tiwanaku itself was long thought to have been founded around 300 BCE. In 2012, however, **Erik Marsh** re-examined the site's earliest radiocarbon dates in the journal *Radiocarbon* and found many of them unreliable. His Bayesian models suggest a founding around 110 CE (roughly 50–170 CE). Most scholars place Tiwanaku's heyday between about 500 and 1000 CE, with the city declining by the 1100s.

## Pyramids, courts and a famous gate

Most of the ancient city was built of adobe and now lies under the modern town. The stone monuments of the ceremonial core survive. The largest is the **Akapana**, which UNESCO describes as originally built of seven platforms and rising more than 18 metres. Nearby are the **Kalasasaya**, a large rectangular enclosure entered by a monumental stairway, and the **Semi-subterranean Temple**, a sunken court whose walls are studded with carved stone heads.

In the Kalasasaya stands the **Gate of the Sun**, cut from a single block of andesite about 3.8 metres wide. Its frieze shows a front-facing figure holding a staff in each hand, flanked by rows of winged figures. UNESCO notes that the scene has been interpreted as an agricultural calendar, but that reading remains speculative. The gate's position today is not original either: it was set upright again during Bolivia's wars of independence.

Visitors should know that much of what they see has been rebuilt. Vranich points out that in the mid-20th century, heavy-handed restorations reset whole sections of wall to give the site a more monumental look. One result is that Tiwanaku is often described as one of the worst-reconstructed sites in South America.

![A tall, weathered stone statue of a standing figure with a square face, holding objects against its chest, densely carved with small motifs, on a stone plinth](images/tiwanaku-ponce-monolith.jpg)
*The Ponce Monolith, about 3 metres tall, carved in grey andesite and now standing in the Kalasasaya. Photo: P. Hughes, [CC BY 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/), via [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tiwanaku_-_Ponce_monolith.jpg).*

The **Ponce Monolith** was dug up in the Kalasasaya in 1957 by a team under the Bolivian archaeologist Carlos Ponce Sanginés. It holds a *kero*, a drinking cup, in one hand and what seems to be a tray for snuffing mind-altering plants in the other. A Christian cross cut into its shoulder suggests it was buried during colonial campaigns against "idolatry."

## Puma Punku: unfinished, not inexplicable

Southwest of the main core lies **Puma Punku**, a T-shaped platform with a footprint about 167 metres long. Its largest sandstone slab measures about 8 by 4 metres and weighs an estimated 83 tonnes. Around it lie roughly 150 andesite blocks with flat faces, sharp edges and inner right angles. Among them are the much-photographed "H" blocks. Spanish visitors in the 16th and 17th centuries already described it as a wondrous but unfinished building. Since then treasure hunters have ransacked it so thoroughly that none of the blocks remain in their original place.

![Several grey andesite blocks carved into identical H-shaped profiles with recessed niches, lined up on a low sandstone platform](images/tiwanaku-puma-punku-h-blocks.jpg)
*Andesite "H" blocks at Puma Punku. Many blocks are mirror-image copies of each other, which suggests standard designs. Photo: Janikorpi, [CC BY-SA 3.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/3.0/), via [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Puma_Punku_H_profile_walls.JPG).*

The masonry is extraordinary, and that has made Puma Punku a favourite of television shows that credit it to lost races or extraterrestrials. **These claims have no support.** Geochemical studies trace the andesite to quarries near the shores of Lake Titicaca, and one 20-tonne block came from across the lake. The sandstone came from mountains south of the site. The masons fully finished each block, decoration included, before setting it in place. In a 2018 study, Vranich drew on a century and a half of field measurements to make 3D-printed models of 140 andesite blocks and 17 sandstone slabs at 4 percent scale. When his team fitted the pieces together, the building's plan turned out to match earlier local temples at Chiripa and Pucara. Vranich says this undercuts the alien claims, which rest partly on the idea that the design has no local precursors. How the heaviest stones were moved is still debated, and the reconstruction is only partial.

## State, confederation or pilgrimage centre?

What kind of society built all this? Late 20th-century fieldwork showed that Tiwanaku was a real city, not only an empty ceremonial centre. Its influence reached into what are now southern Peru, northern Chile and northwestern Argentina. Scholars disagree on how that power worked. One school sees a centralized state with a capital, colonies and an official religion. In 1996 **Juan Albarracín-Jordan** argued instead for "nested hierarchies" of kin groups, whose local elites were tied together by reciprocity and shared ideology more than by direct control. Others see a pilgrimage centre whose rulers worked more like priests and hosts than kings.

Two kinds of evidence feed the debate. In the Moquegua valley of southern Peru, **Paul Goldstein** describes Tiwanaku colonies, perhaps 10,000 to 20,000 settlers, made up of separate migrant groups who kept their own pottery styles and lived only about 10 km from an outpost of the rival **Wari** state. On the lake itself, divers excavating the **Khoa reef** off the Island of the Sun in 2013 found puma-shaped incense burners, sacrificed young llamas, gold and Spondylus shell from waters off Ecuador, about 2,000 km away. Radiocarbon modelling puts these offerings between about 800 and 960 CE. The team argues that such costly rituals helped hold an early state together. That is an interpretation, though, not proof of how the state was run.

## Raised fields and a contested collapse

Tiwanaku's food supply depended partly on **raised fields**: planting platforms separated by water-filled canals that hold the day's heat and help protect crops from frost. UNESCO counts tens of thousands of such fields in the region. When Bolivian farmers revived the method in the late 20th century, they got higher yields.

The city's end is the most heated question. In 1997 a team including **Michael Binford** and **Alan Kolata** used lake-sediment cores to argue that Lake Titicaca fell 12 to 17 metres during a long dry spell from about 1100 to 1400 CE. In their view, the raised-field economy failed and the state fell with it. **Clark Erickson** called this "neo-environmental determinism" in 1999, and Kolata's team replied that a severe drought after about 1150 CE remained the best explanation. In 2005 **Michael Calaway** re-examined the ice-core and lake data and concluded that no such drought can be inferred from them. The question is still open.

The myths are harder to kill. Starting around 1911, the amateur scholar **Arthur Posnansky** claimed that Tiwanaku was 12,000 to 17,000 years old. In his telling it had been a lake port, and its history unfolded in a scheme of superior and inferior races. The first radiocarbon dates, obtained after his death in 1946, disproved his chronology. As Marsh has shown, though, his ideas live on in pseudo-archaeology and on some site tours.
