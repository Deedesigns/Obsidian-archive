## One of the four great kingdoms

Sometime after the middle of the 3rd century CE, a Manichaean text called the *Kephalaia* put into the mouth of the prophet Mani a short list of the world's superpowers. "There are four great kingdoms in the world," it says: Babylon and Persia, the Romans, the Axumites, and a fourth called Silis, usually taken to mean China. Most modern readers would recognise three of those names. The other belonged to **Aksum**, a kingdom centred in what is now Tigray in northern Ethiopia and in Eritrea, which for much of the first millennium CE controlled the African side of the Red Sea. (The *Kephalaia* was compiled by Mani's followers rather than written by him, so the list tells us what his circle thought, not necessarily his own words.)

## From Yeha to Adulis

Aksum did not appear from nowhere, and its origins are much debated. About 30 miles northeast of the city stands the **Great Temple of Yeha**, built around 700 BCE and dedicated to Almaqah, a moon god of the Sabaeans of southern Arabia. Archaeologists call this phase pre-Aksumite, and many link it to a kingdom known from inscriptions as **D'mt**. How much of this culture came from Arabia and how much grew locally is one of the field's long-running arguments; recent fieldwork has tended to stress the local roots.

The first clear written portrait comes from the *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea*, a Greek merchant's guide from the mid-1st century CE. It describes **Adulis**, a port on the Eritrean coast, and says that from there it was a three-day journey to Coloe, "the first market for ivory," and five more days to "the city of the people called Auxumites," where all the ivory from beyond the Nile was brought. The coast was ruled by a king named **Zoskales**, whom the author calls "miserly in his ways and always striving for more, but otherwise upright, and acquainted with Greek literature." Adulis imported Egyptian cloth, glass, iron, Italian wine and Indian steel, and sent out ivory, tortoiseshell and rhinoceros horn.

## Towers carved from single stones

The most famous monuments of Aksum are its **stelae**, tall granite slabs that marked royal and elite tombs, mostly dating to the 3rd and 4th centuries CE. The finest are carved to look like multi-storey buildings, with false doors, windows and beam-ends in stone. The tallest still standing, often called "King Ezana's Stela," rises more than 23 metres. That name is a later tradition; the people who raised the stelae and the people buried beneath them are not actually known.

The largest of them all lies broken on the ground. The **Great Stela** is about 33 metres long and is estimated to weigh over 500 tonnes; UNESCO describes it as possibly the largest single stone stela people ever tried to set upright. It probably fell during or soon after erection, though the exact moment is unknown. Another stela was taken to Rome as a war trophy during the Italian occupation in 1937, returned to Aksum in 2005 and put back up in 2008.

## A king changes his coins

Aksum began minting its own money around the late 3rd century CE, starting with a king named **Endubis**. Gold coins carried Greek legends for foreign trade, while lower-value coins often used Ge'ez, the local language that later became the language of the Ethiopian Church. Because the coins name their kings, they form the backbone of Aksumite chronology.

They also record a religious revolution. Early gold coins of King **Ezana** show the disc and crescent, symbols of the older gods shared with southern Arabia. Later issues replace them with the cross. Ezana's stone inscriptions shift the same way, from thanking the war god Mahrem to praising "the Lord of Heaven." Church historians credit the change to **Frumentius**, a Christian from the Roman world who, as the story goes, was brought to the Aksumite court as a young captive, became a royal tutor and was later consecrated bishop by Athanasius of Alexandria. A letter from the emperor Constantius II, preserved by Athanasius, is addressed to "Aizanas and Sazanas" and demands that Frumentius be sent to Egypt for re-examination, which places Ezana on the throne around 356.

Exactly when Ezana converted is disputed. Estimates range from around 330 to the 350s, and some numismatists argue for an earlier date based on the weight of his Christian gold. Archaeology backs up the early spread of the faith beyond the court: at **Beta Samati**, a town between Aksum and the Red Sea excavated between 2011 and 2016, a team led by Michael Harrower found a basilica radiocarbon dated to the 4th century, along with a Ge'ez inscription asking Christ to be favourable. It may be among the oldest known churches in sub-Saharan Africa.

## Across the sea, and a slow fade

Aksum's reach crossed the water. Around 518 and again in 525, King **Kaleb** sent armies into Himyar in Yemen, the second time after the Jewish Himyarite king Yusuf (known in Arabic sources as Dhu Nuwas) had persecuted Christians at Najran. The 525 campaign ended Yusuf's rule. Christian accounts of these wars are openly partisan, so historians read them alongside South Arabian inscriptions.

The decline came slowly and probably had several causes. The coastline at Adulis gradually moved away from the city, and by around the 8th century Muslim powers controlled the nearby Dahlak islands, which may have cut Aksum off from the Red Sea trade that had made it rich. UNESCO dates Aksum's political decline to around the 10th century, yet Ethiopian emperors kept coming to be crowned there long afterwards. The city's church of St Mary of Zion is still believed by many Ethiopian Christians to hold the Ark of the Covenant, a claim that cannot be checked, since no outsider is permitted to see the object kept there.
