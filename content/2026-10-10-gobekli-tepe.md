## A hill full of pillars

Göbekli Tepe ("Potbelly Hill") sits on a ridge near Şanlıurfa in southeastern Turkey. Surveyors noticed it in the 1960s, but its importance was recognised only after German archaeologist Klaus Schmidt began excavating in 1995. Beneath the mound lay circular and oval enclosures ringed by massive T-shaped limestone pillars, some over 5 metres tall and weighing several tonnes.

## Astonishingly early

Radiocarbon dates place the oldest construction around 9500 BCE, in the Pre-Pottery Neolithic period. That is thousands of years before Stonehenge or the Egyptian pyramids, and before pottery, writing or the wheel. The builders lived at the very threshold of agriculture; wild cereals and hunted gazelle were still central to their diet.

## Carved animals and figures

Many pillars are decorated with reliefs of foxes, boars, snakes, vultures, scorpions and other animals. Some central pillars have carved arms, hands and belts, suggesting they represent stylized human or supernatural beings. The imagery points to a rich symbolic world, though its exact meaning is unknown.

## Temple, or something more?

Schmidt interpreted the site as a ritual centre where hunter-gatherers gathered for feasts and ceremonies, calling it perhaps the world's first temple. Later research has added nuance: excavators have found evidence of cisterns, grinding tools and possibly houses, suggesting people also lived there. The broader region, including nearby Karahan Tepe, now reveals a whole network of similar sites.

## Why it matters

The old view held that farming came first, then settled villages, then monuments and organised religion. Göbekli Tepe suggests the order may have been more tangled. The need to gather, feed and coordinate large groups for building and ritual may itself have encouraged the domestication of plants. Around 8000 BCE the enclosures were deliberately backfilled or buried, which helped preserve them. The site became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2018.
