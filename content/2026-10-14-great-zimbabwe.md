## Houses of stone

In the hills of southeastern Zimbabwe, near the modern town of Masvingo, stand the ruins of the largest stone settlement built in precolonial southern Africa. Its walls were raised without mortar, from granite blocks split from the surrounding outcrops and laid in neat courses. The name *Zimbabwe* is usually explained as coming from the Shona *dzimba dza mabwe*, "houses of stone," though some scholars prefer *dzimba-hwe*, "venerated houses." "Great" distinguishes it from the hundreds of smaller walled sites, also called zimbabwes, scattered across the region.

The ruins cover several square kilometres and fall into three main groups: the **Hill Complex** on a granite ridge, the **Great Enclosure** on the plain below, and the **Valley Complex** between and around them. Most of the city's people probably lived in houses of daga (earth) and timber that have left far fainter traces than the stone walls.

## Rise of a kingdom

Farming communities lived in the valley long before any monument. Stone building on the hill seems to have begun around the eleventh or twelfth century CE, and the site grew into the capital of a powerful state from about the thirteenth century. Its rise is often linked to the decline of **Mapungubwe**, an earlier kingdom on the Limpopo River, around 1300, though how directly power passed from one to the other is debated.

The builders were ancestors of the Shona-speaking peoples who live in Zimbabwe today. The kingdom's wealth rested on cattle, farming and, above all, control of trade. Gold from the plateau and ivory travelled down the river valleys to Swahili ports such as Sofala, linked to Kilwa further north, and into the Indian Ocean trade. Excavations have turned up Chinese celadon and porcelain, Persian pottery, glass beads and a few coins from the coast, evidence of how far the city's goods reached. Population estimates vary widely: older figures of up to about 18,000 people have been challenged by recent studies suggesting no more than about 10,000.

## The Great Enclosure and the birds

The **Great Enclosure**, probably built during the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, is the most famous structure. Its outer wall runs for about 250 metres and rises to roughly 11 metres, decorated near the top with a chevron pattern. Inside stands the solid **Conical Tower**, about 9 metres high and 5.5 metres across. Its purpose is unknown; suggestions include a symbolic granary, a sign of royal generosity, or a ritual monument, but none can be proven.

The site's best-known artefacts are eight soapstone **Zimbabwe Birds**, carved on top of tall columns and probably once set up in the Hill Complex. They may represent a bird of prey such as the bateleur eagle and may be linked to royal ancestors. Several were removed in the colonial period and later returned, and the bird now appears on Zimbabwe's flag.

## Who lived where? A debate

Archaeologists disagree about how the city worked. One view sees the three complexes as successive royal residences, with power moving from the hill to the Great Enclosure and then the valley as new rulers founded new courts. A rival "structuralist" view holds that they were used at the same time for different purposes: the hill for ritual and rule, the Great Enclosure perhaps for royal women or initiation, and the valley for other elites. New radiocarbon dating, led by researchers such as Shadreck Chirikure, has challenged the older sequence and suggests the occupation was more complicated than either model allows.

## Decline and a stolen history

By the fifteenth century the city was losing its importance, and it was largely abandoned during the sixteenth. Possible causes include exhausted farmland, pasture and firewood, pressure on water, a fall in demand for gold, and a shift of trade northward. Power passed to successor states such as **Mutapa** in the north and **Torwa**, centred on Khami, in the southwest. No single explanation is agreed.

When the German explorer Karl Mauch saw the ruins in 1871, he and later European writers credited them to Phoenicians, the Queen of Sheba or other outsiders, refusing to believe local Africans could have built them. Early diggers stripped away layers that would have helped date the site. David Randall-MacIver in 1905 and Gertrude Caton-Thompson in 1929 showed the city was medieval and African, yet Rhodesia's white-minority government kept pressing archaeologists and guidebooks to blur that conclusion for decades. When the country gained independence in 1980 it took its name from the ruins, and in 1986 Great Zimbabwe became a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
