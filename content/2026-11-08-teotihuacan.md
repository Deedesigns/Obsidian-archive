## A name given by strangers

About fifty kilometres northeast of modern Mexico City stand two enormous pyramids and a straight ceremonial road that later peoples called the Avenue of the Dead. The people who built them left no king lists and no account of what they called their city. The name we use, **Teotihuacan**, is Nahuatl, given by the Mexica (the people often called Aztecs) centuries after the metropolis had burned and faded. UNESCO renders it as "the place where the gods were created"; other writers prefer "where humans become gods." We are hearing later visitors, not the builders.

Between about the first and seventh centuries CE the settlement grew into one of the largest cities in the ancient Americas. Archaeologist René Millon estimated some 75,000 to 125,000 residents at its height; later counts differ. The ceremonial core visitors walk today is only a fraction of the mapped city, about twenty square kilometres in Millon's survey.

## Mountains of stone on a cosmic grid

The city's spine is a long north–south avenue lined up with the landscape, especially Cerro Gordo, the mountain that rises behind the Pyramid of the Moon. Builders even shifted the San Juan River so that it crossed the avenue. Platforms along the way use Teotihuacan's signature profile, **talud-tablero**: a sloping wall topped by a framed vertical panel.

The **Pyramid of the Sun**, finished around 200 CE, stands about 63 metres high today, with a base roughly 215 to 225 metres on a side. Its present outline is not wholly original. Between 1905 and 1910 Leopoldo Batres restored the pyramid and, UNESCO notes, arbitrarily rebuilt a fifth terrace. Excavations in 1971 found a cave beneath it used for fire and water rites; some scholars think that cave helped decide where the pyramid would stand. At the avenue's northern end, the smaller **Pyramid of the Moon** echoes Cerro Gordo. To the south, an enclosed plaza known as the Ciudadela holds the **Temple of the Feathered Serpent**.

## A city of apartment compounds

After the great monuments went up, Teotihuacanos poured labour into housing. The Metropolitan Museum estimates between 2,000 and 2,300 single-storey **apartment compounds**, which sheltered about 85 percent of the population. A typical compound measured about 60 by 60 metres, wrapping rooms around courtyards behind a thick outer wall, and held perhaps 60 to 100 people.

The city was multiethnic. Neighbourhoods have yielded pottery, burials and goods from the Basin of Mexico, Oaxaca, the Gulf Coast and west Mexico. Linda Manzanilla's work at compounds such as Teopancazco shows neighbourhood centres organising crafts, feasts and long-distance caravans. Who ruled this mix is still an open question. Unlike the Classic Maya, Teotihuacan left no deciphered dynastic inscriptions. Some archaeologists see collective leadership; others think individual rulers existed but did not advertise themselves as Maya kings did.

## Serpents under the Ciudadela

The Temple of the Feathered Serpent is a six-level pyramid whose façades still carry stone heads of a plumed serpent, among the earliest known images of a being later associated with the Aztec god Quetzalcoatl. In the 1980s, excavations directed by Rubén Cabrera Castro and Saburo Sugiyama found mass burials of people sacrificed while the pyramid was being built, in the late second or early third century CE.

In 2003 archaeologists located a sealed tunnel beneath it, which Sergio Gómez of Mexico's National Institute of Anthropology and History began exploring in 2009. Radiocarbon dates suggest it was dug around the start of the Common Era, used for at least 250 years, then closed by the Teotihuacanos themselves. It runs nearly 103 metres and drops as much as 17 metres, reaching the water table. Chambers at the far end held jade, Caribbean shells and the bones of jaguars and wolves. Traces of liquid mercury in the floors have been read by Gómez as lakes or rivers in a model of the underworld. He has also suggested the tunnel may have been prepared as a tomb for an early ruler. No royal burial has been confirmed there.

## A long reach, then fire

Teotihuacan's influence ran far beyond the valley. Its pottery, obsidian and talud-tablero architecture appear across central Mexico and into the Maya world, as far as Kaminaljuyu in highland Guatemala. Maya inscriptions at Tikal record that in 378 CE a lord named **Sihyaj K'ahk'** ("Born from Fire") arrived on the same day the local king died. Many scholars read this *entrada* as a Teotihuacan-linked intervention; others stress elite imitation and diplomacy as much as conquest. Lidar mapping has since revealed a smaller copy of Teotihuacan's Ciudadela at Tikal.

Then the centre failed. Along the Avenue of the Dead, the main ritual and administrative buildings were burned, and sculptures in palatial compounds such as Xalla were smashed. Radiocarbon work places this "Big Fire" between about 550 and 625 CE; UNESCO dates the destruction and abandonment toward 650. Manzanilla finds no sign of a foreign army and interprets the burning as an internal revolt, perhaps by neighbourhood elites against the rulers. Other researchers have pointed to drought, or to earthquakes that may have shaken the monuments and worsened unrest. People continued to live around the ruins in later centuries, and sixteenth-century writings say the Aztec ruler Moctezuma still made sacrifices there. The builders' own name for the city, and the language they spoke in its palaces, remain unknown.
