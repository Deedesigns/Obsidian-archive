## A king who changed his name

Akhenaten ruled Egypt for about seventeen years, roughly 1353 to 1336 BCE, late in the 18th Dynasty. He came to the throne as **Amenhotep IV**, a name honouring Amun, king of the gods, whose temple at Karnak in Thebes was one of the richest institutions in the land. Within about five years he had taken a new name, **Akhenaten**, usually translated "beneficial to the Aten". The Aten was the disk of the sun, and the king set out to make it the centre, and eventually the sole focus, of Egypt's state religion.

The change began inside the old system. His earliest monuments still show traditional gods, with special prominence for the falcon-headed sun god Re-Harakhte. Soon, beside Amun's own precinct at Karnak, he was building new temples in which the god appeared not as a person or an animal but as a disk in the sky whose rays end in tiny hands, offering the sign of life to the king and queen.

## Worship in the open sun

Traditional Egyptian rites took place in small, dark sanctuaries deep inside temples. Akhenaten's temples turned this inside out: vast open-air courts filled with offering tables, where food was presented directly in the sunlight. To build fast, his masons used small, uniform sandstone blocks known today as **talatat**. Reliefs reconstructed from thousands of them show the royal family making offerings, and one Karnak temple shows Queen **Nefertiti** as the main officiant before the Aten, an unprecedented role for a queen.

The art changed just as abruptly. Statues of the king, such as the colossi from Karnak, gave him a long face, slanted eyes, full lips, a narrow chest and wide hips. Scholars have long argued over whether this reflects a medical condition (Marfan syndrome is often suggested) or a deliberate religious style. The Egyptologist Peter Dorman, writing for Britannica, considers a theological meaning more plausible, and notes that one of the reign's master sculptors recorded that the king instructed him personally.

## A city for the Aten

In his fifth regnal year Akhenaten founded a new capital on an empty bay of desert on the east bank of the Nile in Middle Egypt. He called it **Akhetaten**, "Horizon of the Aten"; today the site is Tell el-Amarna. Its territory was marked out by sixteen known **boundary stelae** carved into the cliffs. The first proclamation, dated to year 5, insists the god chose the place himself: "No official has ever advised me concerning it," the king declares, and the land belongs to no god, no goddess and no ruler. He also orders a tomb for himself in the eastern mountain. A second proclamation in year 6, reaffirmed in year 8, fixed the limits more firmly. Barry Kemp of the Amarna Project points out that the stelae are often misread as a vow never to leave the city; they actually forbid expanding its borders.

## One god, or the god of one family?

The best-known text of the new faith is the **Great Hymn to the Aten**, whose longest copy survives in the tomb that the courtier Ay, later king himself, prepared at Amarna. It praises the sun for waking the world each dawn, creating the child in the womb, making the Nile for Egypt and rain for foreign lands, and giving peoples their different languages and skins. Only Akhenaten and Nefertiti, the texts imply, truly know the god.

Later in the reign the campaign turned destructive: Amun's name and images were chiselled off monuments, and sometimes even the plural word "gods". Early Egyptologists hailed Akhenaten as the world's first monotheist, and the hymn's imagery has often been compared with **Psalm 104** in the Hebrew Bible. Sigmund Freud went further in *Moses and Monotheism* (1939), speculating that Moses was an Egyptian follower of the Aten; that idea is **speculation with no supporting evidence**. Most scholars today are more cautious. The Aten cult borrowed from older gods such as Re-Harakhte, Shu and Maat, and the king himself stood between the god and everyone else as a divine figure. Britannica's entry on the Aten concludes it was not fully monotheistic and not a direct precursor of Judaism, though how to label it remains debated.

## What ordinary people believed

Some 1,000 excavated houses at Amarna suggest the revolution was thinner at street level. Anna Stevens's survey of household religion lists figurines, vessels decorated with images of Hathor and Bes, and amulets of the protective household gods **Bes** and **Taweret**, alongside shrines to the royal family and the Aten in the gardens of larger houses. People evidently kept turning to familiar protectors for childbirth, illness and danger.

Their lives were also hard. Between 2006 and 2013 the Amarna Project excavated 381 graves in the South Tombs Cemetery, a burial ground for ordinary residents, and found widespread signs of malnutrition, stunted growth, injury and heavy labour. In the North Tombs Cemetery, almost all of the people analysed so far died between about 7 and 25 years old, and researchers think they may have been a workforce tied to stone quarrying.

## Erased from history

After Akhenaten's death the experiment unravelled. His young son Tutankhaten became **Tutankhamun**, the court left Akhetaten, and Tutankhamun's **Restoration Stela** at Karnak described a land in distress where "the gods were turning away from this land" and prayers went unanswered. Under Horemheb and the early Ramesside kings, the Aten temples were dismantled and their blocks reused, and the Amarna kings were left off official king lists. A legal text from the reign of Ramesses II, carved in the Saqqara tomb of an official named Mose, refers to "the time of the enemy of Akhetaten", mentioning the king without using his name.

![Timeline of Akhenaten's reign and its aftermath, about 1353 to 1265 BCE](images/akhenaten-timeline.svg)

The erasure had an unintended effect. Because Akhetaten was abandoned rather than built over, its houses, cemeteries and stelae now offer one of the clearest pictures of daily life in any ancient Egyptian city, and of how far a king's new god really reached.
