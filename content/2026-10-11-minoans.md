## Rediscovery

In 1900, British archaeologist Sir Arthur Evans began excavating at Knossos on the island of Crete. He uncovered a vast building complex and named its builders "Minoans" after King Minos of Greek legend. Their own name for themselves is unknown. The civilization flourished from roughly 3000 to 1450 BCE, peaking in the Middle and Late Bronze Age.

## Palaces and towns

The great "palaces" at Knossos, Phaistos, Malia and Zakros were multi-storey complexes arranged around central courtyards, with storerooms, workshops, shrines and light wells. They likely served as administrative, religious and economic centres. Minoan towns had paved roads and advanced drainage systems.

## Art and religion

Minoan frescoes show dolphins, lilies, processions and the famous **bull-leaping** scenes, in which young athletes vault over charging bulls. Figurines of women holding snakes, sacred symbols such as the double axe (*labrys*) and "horns of consecration," and rituals on mountain peaks and in caves suggest a religion in which female figures were prominent.

## Writing

The Minoans used a script known as **Linear A**, which remains undeciphered. Later, Mycenaean Greeks adapted it into Linear B, which Michael Ventris deciphered in 1952 as an early form of Greek.

## Seafarers

Crete sat at the crossroads of the eastern Mediterranean. Minoan trade and influence reached the Cyclades, Egypt and the Levant; Minoan-style frescoes even appear at Avaris in Egypt.

## Decline

The volcanic eruption of Thera (Santorini), dated to roughly the 17th or 16th century BCE, buried the Minoan-influenced town of Akrotiri and probably disrupted the region. But Minoan palaces continued for some time afterward. Around 1450 BCE many sites were destroyed, and Mycenaean Greeks took control of Knossos. Some have linked the Minoans and Thera to the legend of Atlantis, but this remains speculation.
