## A religion without a book

Mithraism is one of the best-documented and worst-understood religions of the ancient world. Archaeologists have found the cult's meeting places from Hadrian's Wall to the Euphrates: roughly 420 sites, about 1,000 inscriptions and some 700 images of its central scene, according to the historian Manfred Clauss. Yet, as Roger Beck wrote in the *Encyclopaedia Iranica*, "no ancient literary works about Mithraism and no substantial sacred texts from Mithraism have survived." Two ancient histories of the cult, by writers named Euboulus and Pallas, are lost.

What remains is mostly stone, paint and graffiti, plus hostile or passing remarks from outsiders. Almost everything we say about what Mithraists *believed* is reconstruction, and scholars disagree about a great deal of it.

## The god in the cave

The Romans called it "the mysteries of Mithras" or "the mysteries of the Persians." Its members met not in temples with columns and open-air altars, like other Roman gods, but in long, windowless rooms built to imitate a cave. Raised benches ran along both sides for a shared meal; a typical room held perhaps 15 to 30 diners. The philosopher Porphyry, writing in the third century CE, said the cave was meant as "an image of the world," and that Zoroaster himself had first dedicated one to Mithras. Modern scholars doubt that founding story, but it shows how Mithraists saw themselves.

At the far end stood the cult image, the **tauroctony** (Greek for "bull-killing"). It is remarkably consistent. Mithras, in trousers, a cloak and a soft Phrygian cap, kneels on a bull and stabs it while looking back over his shoulder toward the Sun. A dog and a snake reach for the blood, a scorpion grips the bull's genitals, a raven watches, and the bull's tail often turns into ears of wheat. Two torchbearers, Cautes with his torch raised and Cautopates with his lowered, stand on either side, with the Sun and Moon above.

Other scenes filled out a story we can only read from pictures. Mithras was born as a young man from a rock, holding a dagger and a torch. He hunted and captured the bull, made the Sun kneel to him, shook hands with him and then feasted with him on the bull's hide. Some reliefs could be turned on a pivot to show the bull-killing on one side and the banquet on the other, which suggests the two scenes framed the cult's own meals.

## Ravens, lions and fathers

Members called themselves *syndexioi*, "those united by the handshake." The Christian writer Jerome lists seven grades of initiation, and a floor mosaic in the Mithraeum of Felicissimus at Ostia, Rome's port, shows their symbols in seven panels, each tied to a planet: Raven (Mercury), Bridegroom (Venus), Soldier (Mars), Lion (Jupiter), Persian (Moon), Sun-runner (Sun) and Father (Saturn). Wall paintings at Capua show small, naked, blindfolded initiates being led through ordeals, and a pit at Carrawburgh on Hadrian's Wall may have been used for tests of endurance.

How universal the ladder was is debated. Clauss noted that only about 14 percent of Mithraic inscriptions before 250 CE name a grade, and argued that the grades may have marked a priestly elite; Richard Gordon and others think most members held one. Surviving membership lists name only men, and most scholars conclude the cult excluded women, though a few recent studies question that.

The membership was distinctive. Inscriptions point to soldiers, customs officers, minor imperial officials, merchants, freedmen and slaves: people with a stake in the imperial order. The cult was strongest in Rome and Ostia and along the Rhine and Danube frontiers, and much weaker in the Greek-speaking East.

## Persian god or Roman invention?

The biggest argument is about origins. The name is undeniably Iranian: Mithra was an old Indo-Iranian god of contracts and oaths, invoked in a Hittite-Mitanni treaty of about 1400 BCE and praised in a Zoroastrian hymn. The Belgian scholar Franz Cumont, who founded modern Mithraic studies around 1900, argued that the Roman cult was essentially Persian religion carried west.

At a congress in Manchester in 1971, John Hinnells and Richard Gordon attacked that model. No Iranian text shows Mithra killing a bull, and the Roman images, they argued, should be read on their own terms. Since then most specialists have favoured some form of reinvention: a new mystery cult created around the late first century CE, perhaps in Rome, by people who knew a little about the East. Beck stresses that there is "still no consensus," and he himself suggested a link to the royal court of Commagene in eastern Anatolia, where Mithras was honoured alongside the Sun. Other clues are tantalising but uncertain. Plutarch says the Cilician pirates defeated in 67 BCE performed "secret rites" of Mithras, and small clay plaques from Kerch in Crimea show a bull-killer who may or may not be Mithras.

A bolder theory, from David Ulansey, reads the tauroctony as a star map: the bull, dog, snake, raven and scorpion match constellations, and the cult began as a response to the discovery of the precession of the equinoxes. Beck accepts that the astral symbolism is real but criticised reading the scene as a literal chart. Treat the star-map origin as an interesting hypothesis, not settled fact.

## The temple under the City of London

The firmest evidence for the cult's chronology comes from the ground. Dedications appear in Rome and on the Danube around 80 to 120 CE and multiply from the mid-second century. In September 1954, builders in the City of London uncovered a mithraeum beside the buried River Walbrook, built around 240 CE. Its finds included a marble tauroctony dedicated by Ulpius Silvanus, a veteran of the Second Legion, and fine marble heads of Mithras, Minerva and Serapis that had been carefully buried. The ruin was moved, rebuilt inaccurately in 1962, and returned near its original spot beneath Bloomberg's European headquarters in 2017.

## Rival to Christianity?

Christian writers noticed the resemblance to their own rites. Around 150 CE Justin Martyr complained that "the wicked devils have imitated" the Eucharist "in the mysteries of Mithras," where bread and a cup of water were set before initiates. In 1882 Ernest Renan famously suggested that if Christianity had faltered, "the world would have been Mithraic." Most historians now think that overstated: the cult was made of small, all-male groups with no central authority and no missionary drive.

Its end came in the fourth century. Emperors still honoured Mithras as "patron of their empire" on an altar at Carnuntum around 308, but dedications soon dwindle. Some temples were wrecked, others were filled in or built over by churches, as at San Clemente in Rome. Coins at Sarrebourg in France show one mithraeum in use until near the end of the century. After that, the god in the cave fell silent.
