## A mound on the Konya plain

On the flat Konya plain of south-central Turkey, two low hills rise up to about 20 metres above the fields. Under the taller, eastern one lie the remains of **Çatalhöyük** (roughly "chah-tahl-hew-yook"), one of the largest and best-preserved Neolithic settlements ever found. UNESCO, which listed it as a World Heritage Site in 2012, describes "a unique streetless settlement of houses clustered back to back with roof access into the buildings."

The British archaeologist **James Mellaart** identified the site in 1958 and dug there from 1961 until 1965, when Turkish authorities withdrew his permit over the separate "Dorak Affair," a scandal about missing Bronze Age artefacts. He was never formally charged, but the mound then sat largely untouched for almost 30 years. In 1993 one of Mellaart's former students, **Ian Hodder**, reopened it, and his Çatalhöyük Research Project ran until 2018, with teams of specialists sieving soil, studying bones and analysing plaster.

How old is it? UNESCO's listing gives 18 levels of occupation on the East Mound between about 7400 and 6200 BCE. A later Bayesian analysis of the radiocarbon dates, used in recent studies, puts the Neolithic town at about **7100 to 5950 BCE**, some 1,150 years of continuous life. A smaller Chalcolithic (copper-age) settlement on the West Mound followed; its end date varies by source, from the mid-sixth millennium to about 5200 BCE.

## Living on the rooftops

There were no streets. Houses were built wall to wall, and people climbed down into them by ladder through a hole in the roof. Inside, walls and floors were coated in white clay plaster and replastered again and again. When a house reached the end of its life, often after several decades, its roof and walls were largely taken down and a new one was built on the same footprint, so the mound grew upward like a layer cake.

At its height, in what researchers call the Middle Period (about 6700 to 6500 BCE), the town may have held roughly **3,500 to 8,000 people**, according to a 2019 review of its human remains in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*. They grew emmer and einkorn wheat, barley, peas and lentils, and herded mostly sheep and goats, with domestic cattle appearing later. Life was not idyllic: the same study found animal and human dung close to homes, parasite eggs, tooth decay from a starchy diet, and healed skull fractures in 25 of 93 skulls studied, which the authors suggest may have come from clay sling balls.

## Painted walls and the "oldest map"

Çatalhöyük became famous for its art: wall paintings of hunters around huge wild bulls and deer, vultures swooping over headless bodies, plaster reliefs of leopards, and bull horns set into walls and benches. People also buried their dead beneath house floors, sometimes later reopening graves to remove skulls. In 2004 Hodder's team found a woman buried holding a skull coated with plaster and painted red.

One painting from Level VII, about 3 metres wide, shows a band of about 80 small rectangles beneath a two-peaked shape. Mellaart read it as a town plan with the twin-peaked volcano **Hasan Dağı**, about 130 km away, erupting above it. That would make it one of the oldest known maps. Other scholars, notably Stephanie Meece in 2006, argued that the "volcano" is a leopard skin above a geometric pattern. In 2014 geologists dated pumice from Hasan Dağı's summit to an eruption about **6960 BCE, give or take 640 years**, overlapping with the town's occupation. That shows people could have seen an eruption; it cannot prove what the painter meant.

A newer controversy hangs over some of the art. In 2018 the geoarchaeologist Eberhard Zangger reported finding, in Mellaart's London flat after his death in 2012, engravings on schist with motifs Mellaart had published as reconstructed Çatalhöyük murals, along with evidence that he had forged documents unrelated to the site. Some murals Mellaart published survive only as his drawings, so those are now treated with suspicion. Zangger himself stressed that the excavated objects are genuine, and the volcano painting was photographed in place when it was found.

## A goddess, or not?

Mellaart believed Çatalhöyük worshipped a **mother goddess**. His star example, found in 1961, is a baked-clay figurine of a heavy-bodied woman seated between two feline armrests. Hodder has questioned whether such figurines were deities at all, and later excavators pointed to phallic imagery and bull skulls as signs that the picture was more mixed. Earlier bone-chemistry studies found that men and women ate similar diets, and many researchers came to see the town as broadly egalitarian.

Then, in 2025, a team led by geneticist Mehmet Somel published ancient DNA from 131 people buried in 35 houses in *Science*. Within houses, genetic links ran mainly through mothers: the authors estimate that 70 to 100 percent of the time daughters stayed connected to a house, while adult sons may have moved elsewhere. Girls were buried with about five times as many grave goods as boys. In early phases, people buried together were often close relatives; in later phases they were often unrelated, which the authors suggest may reflect fostering or adoption.

This is not proof of a matriarchy, as outside experts quoted in *Science* stressed, and it sits awkwardly beside an older tooth-shape study that had suggested women moved in from elsewhere. But it does hint that the families of Çatalhöyük were organised in ways quite different from many later Neolithic societies in Europe, which DNA studies suggest tended to trace descent through men.

## Why it ended

In its final centuries the town shrank. Birth rates appear to have fallen, workloads and herding distances grew, and people seem to have dispersed, with a new settlement rising nearby on the West Mound. Researchers have suggested a drying climate, overused local wood and grazing, and social change as contributing causes, but there is no single agreed explanation. What remains is an unusually detailed record of how some of the world's first farmers learned, at real cost, to live together in large numbers.
