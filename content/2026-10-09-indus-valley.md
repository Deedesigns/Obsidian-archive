## A forgotten giant

Between roughly 2600 and 1900 BCE, the largest urban civilization of its time flourished along the Indus River and its neighbours in what are now Pakistan and northwest India. It is often called the Harappan civilization after Harappa, the first site excavated in the 1920s. Its territory was larger than that of Egypt and Mesopotamia combined, yet it was largely unknown until modern archaeology uncovered it.

## Planned cities

Cities such as **Mohenjo-daro**, Harappa, Dholavira and Rakhigarhi show remarkable planning. Streets were laid out in rough grids, houses were built from standardized baked bricks, and many homes had private bathing areas connected to covered drains. Mohenjo-daro's "Great Bath," a watertight brick pool, may have served a ritual purpose. Notably, archaeologists have found no obvious palaces or grand royal tombs, which has led to debate about how these cities were governed.

## Trade and craft

Indus people used a standardized system of cubical stone weights and produced fine beads of carnelian, shell ornaments and copper tools. Their goods reached Mesopotamia, where texts mention a land called *Meluhha*, which most scholars identify with the Indus region. The port at Lothal in Gujarat may have had a dockyard.

## The undeciphered script

Thousands of small square stamp seals, often carved with animals such as a one-horned "unicorn" bull, carry short inscriptions in the Indus script. Because most inscriptions are only a few signs long and no bilingual text has been found, the script remains undeciphered, and even the language it recorded is unknown.

## Decline

From about 1900 BCE, the large cities were gradually abandoned and populations shifted to smaller villages further east. Older ideas of a violent "Aryan invasion" are no longer supported by the evidence. Most researchers now point to a combination of factors:

- Shifting monsoon patterns and a drier climate
- Changes in river courses, including the drying of the Ghaggar-Hakra system
- Disruption of long-distance trade with Mesopotamia
