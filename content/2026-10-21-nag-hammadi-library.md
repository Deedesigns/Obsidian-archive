## A jar at the foot of a cliff

Late in 1945, probably in December, men from a village near **Nag Hammadi** in Upper Egypt were digging at the foot of the **Jabal al-Tarif**, a cliff riddled with ancient tombs, when they found a sealed jar. Inside were twelve papyrus books bound in leather, plus eight loose leaves from a thirteenth. Today they are known as the **Nag Hammadi codices**, and they are kept in the **Coptic Museum** in Cairo.

The books hold 52 tractates, or separate works, written in **Coptic**, the last stage of the Egyptian language, set down in a mostly Greek-derived alphabet. Almost all are translations of Greek originals, and most were unknown before 1945. Several survive nowhere else.

## The trouble with the discovery story

The tale usually told comes from the American scholar **James M. Robinson**, who tracked down the finder, **Muhammad 'Ali al-Samman**, in the 1970s. It is a vivid story: a farmer digging for fertile soil, fear that the jar held a jinn, a blood feud and a murder, and a mother who burned some pages in her oven.

Scholars now treat that story with care. In a 2013 article, **Mark Goodacre** showed that Robinson's own versions disagree, with two, seven or eight people present at the find, and that Muhammad 'Ali told it differently again on camera in 1987. Goodacre concluded that the plainer account published by the French scholar **Jean Doresse** in 1958 may be more reliable. What is secure is the outline: a local find, codices that passed through dealers' hands, and a slow gathering of the books into the Coptic Museum in the 1950s.

One book got away. Most of **Codex I** was taken out of Egypt by a dealer and bought in 1952 for the C. G. Jung Institute in Zurich, earning it the name **Jung Codex**. Its leaves were returned to Cairo only in 1975, once a first edition had been published.

## What the books contain

The best-known text is the **Gospel of Thomas**, in Codex II: about 114 sayings attributed to Jesus, with no birth story, miracles or crucifixion. It opens: "These are the secret sayings which the living Jesus spoke and which Didymos Judas Thomas wrote down" (translation by Thomas O. Lambdin). Some sayings match ones in Matthew, Mark and Luke; others are wholly new.

The find solved an old puzzle. In 1897 and 1903, the British papyrologists **Bernard Grenfell** and **Arthur Hunt** had found Greek fragments of unknown sayings of Jesus at **Oxyrhynchus**, farther down the Nile. After 1945 scholars realized these were pieces of the Greek Gospel of Thomas.

When Thomas was written is hotly debated. Most scholars place it in the 2nd century CE, drawing on older sayings; a minority argue for parts of it as early as the 1st century, and others for a date after about 170.

The rest of the library is just as varied. There is the **Gospel of Truth**, a meditation linked to the followers of the 2nd-century teacher **Valentinus**; three copies of the **Secret Book of John**, a myth in which a flawed lesser god makes the material world; writings in the tradition of **Hermes Trismegistus**; and even a loose Coptic version of a passage from **Plato's Republic**.

## Were they "Gnostic"?

The texts are often called "Gnostic gospels," after the Greek *gnosis*, knowledge: salvation comes through secret knowledge of a true God beyond the world's maker. Many contain such ideas, but the label is contested. In *Rethinking "Gnosticism"* (1996), **Michael Williams** argued that it lumps together groups with very different beliefs, and many specialists now prefer more specific names, such as Valentinian or Sethian, or no label at all. The Teachings of Silvanus, for example, reads as ordinary moral advice.

## Monks, a bishop and a grave?

Who owned these books, and why were they buried? Scraps of used papyrus pasted inside the leather covers as stiffening hold clues. Some in the cover of **Codex VII** carry dates of 341, 346 and 348 CE, so that cover cannot be older than 348. Others are letters among monks.

That points toward the monasteries founded by **Pachomius** from about 320 CE, which lay nearby. Robinson suggested that monks hid the books after 367, when **Athanasius**, bishop of Alexandria, issued his **39th Festal Letter**. It listed the 27 books of today's New Testament and warned against "apocryphal" writings as "an invention of heretics."

That link to the letter is a hypothesis, not a recorded event. **Hugo Lundhaug** and **Lance Jenott** (2015) argue that monks, most likely Pachomians, did produce and read the codices in the 4th and 5th centuries. **Nicola Denzey Lewis** and **Justine Ariel Blount** (2014) counter that private Christians may have commissioned them and been buried with them, following an old Egyptian custom of placing books in graves. The question is open.

## Why it matters

Before 1945, most of what was known about these groups came from their opponents, church writers such as Irenaeus who quoted them in order to refute them. Nag Hammadi let these voices speak for themselves, and that is why a jar of books from a cliff in Upper Egypt still shapes how early Christianity is studied.
