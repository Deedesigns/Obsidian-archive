## A people without their own name

Along the hot, humid lowlands of southern Veracruz and Tabasco, in what is now Mexico, the first elaborate civilization of Mesoamerica flourished from roughly 1200 to 400 BCE. We call these people the **Olmec**, but that is not their name. It comes from the Aztec (Nahuatl) word *Olmecatl*, "rubber people," because the region was famous for rubber made from the latex of local trees. What the Olmec called themselves, and what language they spoke, are unknown.

Their main centres were **San Lorenzo**, **La Venta**, **Laguna de los Cerros** and **Tres Zapotes**. Modern study of them really began when Matthew Stirling, working with the Smithsonian Institution and National Geographic, excavated at Tres Zapotes in 1938 and then at La Venta and San Lorenzo in the 1940s. Radiocarbon dating in the 1950s showed that the sites were older than the Maya.

## The colossal heads

The Olmec are best known for their **colossal heads**: seventeen are usually counted, though some lists give sixteen, ranging from about 1.5 to 3.4 metres tall. The first was found by chance at Tres Zapotes in 1862. Each was carved from basalt quarried in the Tuxtla Mountains and then moved, by sledge, raft or both, over dozens of kilometres of swamp and river. San Lorenzo Colossal Head 1, found by Stirling in 1945, stands 2.84 metres high and weighs about 25 tonnes.

Every face is different, and each helmet-like headdress has its own emblem. Most scholars therefore read the heads as portraits of particular rulers, perhaps with name or family signs on their headgear. They are usually described as men, but art historians such as Billie Follensbee point out that they show no clearly male or female features, so that assumption is now questioned. Some heads also carry traces of earlier carving. The art historian James Porter argued in 1989 that at least some were **recarved from older thrones**, which may also explain why many San Lorenzo monuments look deliberately damaged. Damage once read as a sign of revolt may instead be part of a long cycle of recarving and reuse. The debate is not settled.

## La Venta and the stone thrones

After San Lorenzo declined, probably in the tenth century BCE, **La Venta** in Tabasco became the leading centre until about 400 BCE. With little local stone, its builders worked mostly in earth and clay. Its Great Pyramid rises more than 30 metres and holds an estimated 100,000 cubic metres of fill. Beneath its plazas, excavators found huge buried "pavements," one made of nearly 500 serpentine blocks that were laid down as offerings and then covered over. Much of the ancient city has since been damaged by modern development, including an oil refinery, and many of its monuments now stand in a museum park in Villahermosa.

![A massive rectangular stone monument with a seated figure emerging from a niche beneath a carved ledge](images/olmec-la-venta-altar-4.jpg)

The great boxy stones that early explorers called "altars" are now widely thought to be **thrones**. One piece of evidence is a painting in Oxtotitlan Cave, far away in Guerrero, which shows a richly dressed figure seated on just such a block.

## Rubber, jade and sacred places

Olmec religion has to be pieced together from images and offerings. At **El Manatí**, a spring and bog near San Lorenzo discovered in 1987, people left offerings from roughly 1600 to 1200 BCE: wooden busts, stone axes, the bones of infants, and the **oldest rubber balls yet found**, early evidence of the Mesoamerican ballgame. Olmec art is full of composite creatures and "were-jaguar" or baby-faced figures, part human and part animal. Interpreting them as specific gods, as some scholars do, means reading backward from much later Maya and Aztec religion, which is useful but risky.

## Mother culture or sister culture?

Olmec-style objects turn up from the Valley of Mexico to Central America, and scholars have long argued over what that means. The **"mother culture"** view holds that the Olmec invented much of what later became Mesoamerican civilization and spread it outward. The **"sister cultures"** view sees many regions developing together and sharing older religious ideas. In 2005 a chemical study of pottery led by Jeffrey Blomster concluded that San Lorenzo exported its wares widely but imported none, which seemed to favour the mother-culture side; other archaeologists challenged both the method and the conclusions.

Writing is just as contested. The **Cascajal Block**, a serpentine slab carrying 62 signs, was found by road builders in the late 1990s and published in *Science* in 2006 as possibly the oldest writing in the Americas, from around 900 BCE. Because it came from a pile of debris rather than a dated layer, and because nothing else like it is known, some archaeologists doubt the date, the reading as writing, or both. The later Isthmian or "epi-Olmec" script of the region is better attested.

## Fading away

By around 400 BCE La Venta was in decline and the distinctive Olmec style faded, although people kept living in the region and Tres Zapotes carried on. Why is unclear: changes in river courses, environmental stress, political rivalry and shifting trade have all been suggested, and several may have worked together. One claim that keeps resurfacing, that the colossal heads show African visitors, is rejected by archaeologists: the faces match the features of Indigenous people of the region, and there is no evidence of ancient transatlantic contact.
