## A kingdom upriver

South of Egypt's First Cataract at Aswan, the Nile winds through the desert of **Nubia**, today northern Sudan and southern Egypt. Egyptian texts called it **Kush**, a source of gold, ivory and soldiers. The British Museum describes the region as home to the earliest urban culture of sub-Saharan Africa, and its rulers repeatedly rivalled, borrowed from and even ruled Egypt.

Historians usually describe not one Kush but a sequence of kingdoms: **Kerma**, then the **Napatan** kingdom, then the kingdom of **Meroë**, stretching in all from about 2500 BCE to the 4th century CE.

## Kerma, the first Kush

By about 2500 BCE a powerful state had grown up around the town of **Kerma**, just above the Nile's Third Cataract. Excavations led since 1977 by **Charles Bonnet** of the University of Geneva have mapped a walled city of more than 30 hectares with round buildings and a huge mud-brick temple known as a *deffufa*. Kerma's rulers were buried under great earthen mounds, accompanied by human sacrifices, and its potters made some of the finest wares in the Nile Valley.

During Egypt's Second Intermediate Period, in roughly the 17th and 16th centuries BCE, Kerma controlled the Nile as far north as Aswan. An intercepted letter from the Hyksos king Apophis even urged the ruler of Kush to join him against Thebes. The Theban kings struck back, and under **Thutmose I**, around 1500 BCE, Egypt conquered Kerma and ruled Nubia through a viceroy for roughly four centuries.

## Pharaohs from the south

When Egyptian power withdrew at the end of the New Kingdom, local chiefdoms filled the gap. By the 8th century BCE their heirs ruled from **Napata**, beneath **Jebel Barkal**, a flat-topped sandstone hill that Egyptians had believed was a home of the god Amun.

Kushite kings had already taken over parts of Upper Egypt when, in about 728 BCE, King **Piye** sailed north to crush a coalition of Delta princes. His granite **Victory Stela**, set up at Jebel Barkal in his 21st regnal year, offers besieged towns a stark choice. In Robert Ritner's translation: "Open, you will live; close, you will die." The text also shows Piye more upset to find a defeated rival's horses starving than by any of his other crimes.

Piye and his successors ruled Egypt as its **Twenty-fifth Dynasty**. Their dates are debated, and some scholars argue for a shorter chronology than the usual one. The best-known, **Taharqa** (690 to 664 BCE), built across Egypt and Nubia and appears in the Hebrew Bible as "Tirhakah, king of Ethiopia." But meddling in the Levant drew the attention of Assyria. Assyrian armies drove Taharqa out of Egypt, and his successor **Tantamani** lost it for good in about 664 to 656 BCE.

## A land of pyramids

Piye revived a custom Egypt had long abandoned: burying kings under pyramids. Taharqa founded a new royal cemetery at **Nuri**, where his pyramid, about 52 metres square at the base, was the largest ever built in Sudan. Kushite pyramids are smaller and steeper than Egypt's, with burials beneath them rather than inside, and there are far more of them. Guinness World Records counts at least 230 surviving in Sudan, against roughly 120 to 140 in Egypt.

The last king buried at Nuri, **Nastasen**, died in about 308 BCE. From the 3rd century BCE most royal burials moved far upstream to **Meroë**, between the Nile and the Atbara, which UNESCO describes as the kingdom's heartland.

## Meroë: script, iron and Rome

Meroë's culture blended Egyptian, Greek, Roman and local African elements. Its people worshipped Amun alongside their own lion-headed god **Apedemak**, and they wrote their own language in two scripts of 23 signs, one hieroglyphic and one cursive. The British Egyptologist **F. L. Griffith** worked out the sound values of the signs between 1909 and 1911, so Meroitic can be read aloud. But with no bilingual text like the Rosetta Stone, it still cannot be fully translated. Claude Rilly has since shown that it belongs to the Northern East Sudanic branch of the Nilo-Saharan family, related to Nubian, which may help.

Meroë's huge mounds of iron slag led the scholar Archibald Sayce to call it "the Birmingham of Africa" in 1912. Research led by **Jane Humphris** since 2012 suggests the furnaces ran for more than a thousand years, burning charcoal made almost entirely from Nile acacia. The charcoal shows no sign of the fuel crisis or deforestation once blamed for Meroë's decline.

The kingdom was also willing to fight Rome. According to the Greek geographer **Strabo**, a Kushite army raided Roman Egypt in about 25 BCE, captured Aswan and Philae and threw down statues of Augustus. The Roman prefect **Petronius** struck back as far as Napata. Strabo names the enemy ruler only as **Candace**, really the Meroitic royal title *kandake*, and calls her "a masculine woman, and who had lost an eye." Many scholars identify her with Queen **Amanirenas**, though that is not certain. The peace was favourable to Kush. At Samos, Augustus gave her envoys "all that they desired" and cancelled the tribute he had imposed. A bronze head of Augustus, now in the British Museum, was found buried beneath the steps of a temple at Meroë, probably so that people would walk over the emperor.

## Twilight

Meroë faded in the 4th century CE, as trade shifted to the Red Sea kingdom of **Aksum** in Ethiopia and new groups such as the Noba moved into the region. An inscription of the Aksumite king **Ezana** describes a campaign that reached the meeting of the Nile and the Atbara in about the 350s CE, and it was long read as Meroë's death blow. Some scholars, such as Michael Zach, argue that Aksumite forces had invaded earlier in the century and that Meroë limped on for decades afterward as a vassal state. By the 6th century the Nubian kingdoms that followed had become Christian. Its pyramids still stand, and some 2,000 Meroitic inscriptions are still waiting to be fully read.
