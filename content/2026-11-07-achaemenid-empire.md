## From Anshan to a world empire

In about 559 BCE a Persian king named **Cyrus II**, later called Cyrus the Great, took the throne of Anshan in what is now Fars in southwestern Iran. Within a decade he had defeated his Median overlord **Astyages** (about 550), then turned west against wealthy Lydia under Croesus. Sardis fell, and the Greek cities of Ionia came under Persian control. In 539 Persian forces defeated the Babylonian army at Opis; Cyrus entered Babylon and presented himself as a traditional Mesopotamian king, restoring temples and releasing political prisoners. His son **Cambyses II** completed the western arc by conquering Egypt in 525. At its height the empire stretched from the Aegean and Egypt to the Indus and Central Asia—the largest political unit the ancient world had yet known. Much of what we know comes from Greek writers, above all Herodotus; Babylonian chronicles, royal inscriptions and archaeology give a less Athens-centred view of the same empire.

## Babylon and the Cyrus Cylinder

The most famous document of Cyrus's Babylonian policy is the **Cyrus Cylinder**, a barrel-shaped clay foundation text found in Babylon in 1879 and now in the British Museum. Written in Akkadian in the idiom of Babylonian royal propaganda, it presents Cyrus as the chosen tool of the city god Marduk, who restores neglected cults and returns deported peoples and their gods. The British Museum stresses that calling the cylinder a "charter of human rights" projects a modern idea onto an ancient foundation deposit; what it does show is a deliberate claim to legitimate, restorative kingship. The biblical books of Ezra and Isaiah remember Cyrus as the liberator who allowed Judean exiles to return and rebuild the Temple in Jerusalem—another face of the same imperial flexibility.

## Darius, Behistun and the satrapies

Cambyses died in 522 amid a succession crisis. **Darius I**, a Persian noble who claimed descent from the eponymous ancestor Achaemenes, seized the throne. Whether the rival he killed was Cyrus's brother Bardiya or an impostor named Gaumata is still debated; Darius's own version is carved high on a cliff at **Bīsotūn (Behistun)** in Old Persian, Elamite and Babylonian. The relief shows the king before a line of bound rebels, under the symbol of Ahura Mazdā. Scholars note that contemporary evidence calling Cyrus and Cambyses "Achaemenids" is thin; Darius may have retroactively knitted two elite families into one dynastic story.

![Rock relief and trilingual inscription of Darius I high on the cliff face at Behistun, Iran](images/achaemenid-behistun-inscription.jpg)
*The Behistun relief and trilingual inscription of Darius I, carved high above the ancient road in Kermanshah Province, Iran. Photo: Hara1603, public domain, via [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Bisotun_Iran_Relief_Achamenid_Period.JPG).*

Once the revolts were crushed, Darius reorganised the empire into **satrapies**—provinces under governors (*satraps*, "protectors of the kingdom") who collected tribute, dispensed justice and could lead armies, while royal inspectors (remembered in Greek sources as the "king's eyes" or "ears") checked their loyalty. Imperial Aramaic became the bureaucracy's common language from Egypt to northwest India. Darius standardised coinage with the gold **daric**, fostered roads and canals, added northwestern India to the realm, and about 518 began the terrace and palaces of **Persepolis**.

## Roads, capitals and an imperial stage

The so-called **Royal Road** linked Susa with Sardis across roughly 2,400–2,600 kilometres of relays and stations, so that royal couriers could cross the empire in days rather than months. Susa served as the main administrative capital; Babylon, Ecbatana and Persepolis hosted the court by season and ceremony. Persepolis itself was less a day-to-day government centre than a monumental stage: Apadana audience hall, treasury and endless reliefs of soldiers and tribute-bearers enacting the harmony of many peoples under one king. Craftsmen and materials listed in Darius's Susa building inscription came from Lydia, Egypt, Bactria, India and beyond—an empire made visible in stone and glaze.

## Greece, crisis and Alexander

Darius's attempt on mainland Greece ended in defeat at **Marathon** in 490. His son **Xerxes I** returned in 480 with a massive land-and-sea force, took Athens, then lost the fleet at Salamis and the army at Plataea in 479. Those failures mattered enormously in Greek memory, and they ended Persian expansion in the Aegean, but they did not dismantle the empire. For another century and a half Achaemenid gold shaped Greek politics, Egypt slipped in and out of control, and satraps sometimes rebelled. **Artaxerxes III** reconquered Egypt in the 340s; palace murders then brought **Darius III** to the throne just as **Alexander of Macedon** crossed the Hellespont in 334. After Issus (333) and Gaugamela (331), Persepolis was taken and parts of its palaces burned in early 330; Darius III was murdered by his own officers the same year. Alexander claimed the Persian empire as its new Great King—proof, as the Metropolitan Museum notes, of how solid the Achaemenid structure still was even in defeat.
