## A discovery in the caves

In late 1946 or early 1947, Bedouin shepherds searching for a lost goat near Qumran, on the northwest shore of the Dead Sea, entered a cave and found clay jars holding ancient scrolls. Over the next decade, archaeologists and Bedouin searched eleven caves and recovered tens of thousands of fragments, belonging to roughly 900 different manuscripts.

## What the scrolls contain

The texts date mainly from about the third century BCE to the first century CE. Most are written in Hebrew, with others in Aramaic and Greek, chiefly on parchment. They fall into three broad groups:

- **Biblical manuscripts**: copies of every book of the Hebrew Bible except Esther, including the nearly complete Great Isaiah Scroll
- **Other religious writings**: works such as 1 Enoch and Jubilees, known before only in later translations
- **Sectarian texts**: rules and writings of a particular community, such as the Community Rule and the War Scroll

## Why they matter

Before the scrolls, the oldest complete Hebrew Bible manuscripts dated to around 1000 CE. The Qumran copies are about a thousand years older. They show that the biblical text was remarkably stable in many books, while also revealing variant versions that circulated before the text was standardised.

## Who wrote them?

Many scholars have linked the scrolls to the **Essenes**, a Jewish group described by ancient writers such as Josephus, Philo and Pliny the Elder, and connected them to the ruins at Qumran. Others argue the scrolls came from several Jewish groups, perhaps brought from Jerusalem for safekeeping before the Roman destruction of the city in 70 CE. The debate continues.

## Today

Long controversy over slow publication ended in the 1990s, when the full collection became available to scholars. Many scrolls are now held by the Israel Antiquities Authority and the Shrine of the Book in Jerusalem, and high-resolution images are available online through the Leon Levy Dead Sea Scrolls Digital Library.
