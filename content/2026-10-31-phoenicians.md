## A people named by outsiders

Almost everything we think we know about the Phoenicians starts with a word they probably did not use. *Phoinikes* was what Greek writers called the merchants and sailors who came from the coast of what is now Lebanon and southern Syria. The name may be linked to *phoinix*, a Greek word for a red-purple colour, though the etymology is not certain.

The people themselves seem to have thought in terms of cities. In her 2018 book *In Search of the Phoenicians*, the historian **Josephine Quinn** points out that there is no known inscription in which one of them calls himself or herself a "Phoenician," or uses any other collective name. They described themselves by family and by city: a man of **Tyre**, of **Sidon**, of **Byblos** or of **Arwad**. These were rival, often warring, city-kingdoms, each with its own king, gods and artistic tastes. Quinn argues that "Phoenician" was an outsiders' label that later suited the imperial ambitions of Carthage and Rome, and later still of modern nationalists in places as different as Lebanon and Ireland. Quinn doesn't deny that these communities had real ties; she stresses shared religious practices, colonial links and family networks instead of a single ethnic identity. Reviewers have welcomed the challenge while debating how far it goes, and scholars still argue over how much those ties added up to. Keep that debate in mind: "the Phoenicians" is a convenient modern shorthand for a cluster of related port cities.

## Purple from sea snails

The most famous product of this coast was a dye. **Tyrian purple** was made from the glands of predatory sea snails of the murex family, and it took a staggering number of them; when a chemist recreated the pigment in 1909, he processed about 12,000 snails to get 1.4 grams. The colour became a mark of rank in the Roman world.

The industry is older than the name "Phoenician." Between 1969 and 1974, a University of Pennsylvania team under **James Pritchard** dug at Sarafand, ancient **Sarepta**, between Tyre and Sidon. In an industrial quarter of pottery kilns and workshops, beside heaps of crushed *Murex trunculus* shells, they found jar fragments stained purple. Laboratory tests at the Smithsonian confirmed the stain was the real dye, making it the earliest chemically verified royal purple yet known, from the 13th century BCE.

## The letters that travelled west

The cities' most lasting export was a writing system. Phoenician scribes used a compact alphabet of 22 letters, all consonants, which descended from earlier experiments with alphabetic signs in the Levant and Sinai. In 1923 the French archaeologist **Pierre Montet**, working at Byblos, found the stone coffin of a king named **Ahiram**. Around its lid runs an inscription of about 38 words, put up by his son, warning that any king or general who opens the coffin will lose his royal staff and throne.

![A large carved limestone sarcophagus on four crouching lions in a museum, with rows of figures in low relief on its side](images/phoenicians-ahiram-sarcophagus.jpg)
*The sarcophagus of Ahiram, king of Byblos, now in the National Museum of Beirut. Its inscription is the earliest long text in the fully developed Phoenician alphabet. Photo: O.Mustafin, public domain, via [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ahiram_Sarcophagus_1.JPG).*

Its date is a long-running argument. Montet's excavation seemed to point to the 13th century BCE; most scholars, judging by the letter shapes, place the inscription around 1000 BCE; and a minority, including the art historian Edith Porada and the scholar Ronald Wallenfels, argue for the 9th century or later. One suggestion is that the coffin was placed in an older tomb shaft, which would explain the early material around it. Either way, the shapes are clearly the ancestors of the letters the Greeks adopted, probably sometime in the 9th or 8th century BCE. The Greeks themselves remembered the debt. Herodotus, writing in the 5th century BCE, says the Ionians learned their letters from Phoenicians and "gave to these characters the name of Phoenician." Through Greek, and then Etruscan and Latin, the same letters are on this page.

## Tribute, ships and the far west

The cities were rich, and their powerful neighbours knew it. Bronze bands from a gate at Balawat in northern Iraq, made for the Assyrian king **Shalmaneser III** (reigned 859–824 BCE), show porters carrying goods to the Assyrian camp, with a label naming them as gifts from Tyre and Sidon.

![A bronze strip in low relief showing a line of bearded men in long robes carrying bags, trays and bundles, below a line of cuneiform writing](images/phoenicians-balawat-tyre-tribute.jpg)
*Porters bring tribute from Tyre and Sidon to Shalmaneser III, on a fragment of the bronze gate bands from Balawat, 859–824 BCE, now in the Walters Art Museum, Baltimore. Photo: Walters Art Museum, public domain, via [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Assyrian_-_Fragments_of_Bands_from_a_Gate_-_Walters_542335_-_View_A.jpg).*

Part of that wealth came from the sea routes west. Tyrian sailors went after silver and other metals in southern Spain, and settlements took root in Cyprus, Sicily, Sardinia, North Africa and beyond the Strait of Gibraltar at Gadir (modern Cádiz). Radiocarbon dates from Huelva in Spain suggest contact there by the 9th century BCE, earlier than once thought. The greatest of these settlements was **Carthage**, in modern Tunisia. Ancient writers gave its founding as 814 or 813 BCE, and for once archaeology broadly agrees: animal bones from its earliest levels have been radiocarbon dated to roughly 835–800 BCE.

The most daring voyage is the hardest to judge. Herodotus reports that the Egyptian pharaoh Necho II, around 600 BCE, sent Phoenician crews to sail around Africa, a trip he says took nearly three years. He doubted one detail, that the sun appeared on their right as they sailed west. That detail is just what sailors south of the equator would see, which is why some historians take the story seriously. Others remain sceptical, and no physical trace of the voyage has been found.

## The tophet question

At Carthage, and at other western settlements, lie open-air precincts that scholars call **tophets**, borrowing a word for a sacrificial place in the Hebrew Bible. They hold thousands of urns filled with the cremated bones of infants and young animals, often marked by stone stelae dedicated to the gods Baal Hammon and Tanit.

![Several weathered sandstone stelae and small stone shrines standing in front of an old rubble wall](images/phoenicians-carthage-tophet-stelae.jpg)
*Sandstone stelae in the tophet of Salammbô at Carthage. Photo: Pradigue, [CC BY-SA 3.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/3.0/), via [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:St%C3%A8les_tophet_Carthage.jpg).*

Greek and Roman authors accused the Carthaginians of sacrificing their children, and whether the tophets confirm it is one of the fiercest debates in Mediterranean archaeology. In 2010 a team led by the anthropologist **Jeffrey Schwartz** studied 348 urns holding about 540 individuals. They found many babies who died before or around birth, an age pattern they said fits natural infant deaths, and concluded the tophet was a cemetery for the very young, while allowing that some sacrifice may have happened. A team led by **Patricia Smith** examined the same collection, reached different age estimates, and argued in 2011 and 2013 that sacrifice was "very probable." Both teams have kept publishing rebuttals. For now the honest verdict is that the case is unresolved.

## An island stormed

The homeland cities passed under Assyrian, Babylonian and then Persian rule, often keeping their own kings and fleets. The end of Tyre's independence came in 332 BCE, when Alexander the Great built a causeway out to the island city. According to the historian Arrian, the siege lasted seven months, and afterwards about 30,000 Tyrians and foreigners were sold into slavery. Carthage carried on for nearly two more centuries until Rome destroyed it in 146 BCE. The alphabet, however, was already everywhere.
