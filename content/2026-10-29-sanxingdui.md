## Three mounds and a ditch

The Sichuan Basin in southwest China is ringed by mountains, and for most of Chinese history it sat outside the main story of early civilization, which was told from the Yellow River valley. Later texts did remember a kingdom here called **Shu**. The *Chronicles of Huayang*, compiled in the 4th century CE, lists half-legendary Shu kings, among them **Cancong**, said to have had protruding eyes. The kingdom ended for certain in 316 BCE, when the state of Qin conquered it.

In 1927 a farmer and his son, digging a ditch near three earthen mounds outside Guanghan, about 40 kilometres northeast of Chengdu, turned up hundreds of jades. The mounds, called **Sanxingdui** ("three-star mounds"), were later shown to be stretches of a city wall. David Crockett Graham of the West China Union University museum led a first small excavation in 1934. The excavators now date activity at the site from roughly 2700 to 1000 BCE, and radiocarbon dates suggest the walled city was founded around 1600 BCE. Its core covered more than 3 square kilometres, and its sloping earthen walls may have worked as flood dikes as much as defences.

## The pits of 1986

The real shock came in summer 1986, when workers digging clay for a brickyard found two rectangular pits about 20 to 30 metres apart. Together they held some 900 bronze, gold, jade and ivory objects, plus elephant tusks and cowrie shells. Many bronzes had been deliberately broken and burned, then carefully buried.

Nothing like them was known from China. Shang bronze casters at Anyang made ritual vessels for food and wine, decorated mostly with abstract animal masks, and rarely showed human beings at all. Sanxingdui's artists made people. The most famous is a robed figure on a tall pedestal, 260.8 centimetres high and weighing about 180 kilograms, according to the Metropolitan Museum of Art. His huge hands are curled around something that has vanished, perhaps an ivory tusk or a wooden staff. Scholars often call him a priest or a ruler, but nobody knows.

The pits also held dozens of life-size bronze heads, some once covered in gold foil, and masks with angular faces and enormous ears. The strangest masks have eyes that jut out like cylinders. Many writers link them to Cancong's legendary eyes. That is an attractive idea, but it joins objects from around 1100 BCE to a text written some 1,400 years later, so treat it as a guess.

Then there are the trees. A bronze tree from Pit 2, rebuilt from fragments over about a decade, now stands 3.96 metres tall, with nine branches and birds perched on them. Some scholars connect it with the *Fusang* tree of later Chinese myth, where ten suns rested, though again the texts are much later than the bronze. Excavators say at least eight such trees have been found.

## Six more pits

In 2019 archaeologists noticed a square depression next to Pit 2. From 2020 to 2022 a team from the Sichuan Provincial Institute of Cultural Relics and Archaeology and several universities excavated six new pits inside climate-controlled shelters, with an on-site lab. According to the provincial institute, Pits 3 to 8 produced nearly 13,000 numbered objects, about 3,155 of them fairly complete. Finds included a gold mask from Pit 5 weighing about 280 grams, a kneeling bronze figure with a wine vessel on its head, a wooden chest painted with cinnabar, and traces of silk.

The new digs also changed how the old finds look. Fragments from different pits fit together: one composite statue was rebuilt from five pieces found in Pits 2, 3 and 8. That suggests the pits were filled at about the same time, as one event. Unlike Shang founders, who usually cast vessels in a single pour, Sanxingdui craftsmen often cast parts separately and joined them.

## Settling the date

For more than 30 years the age of the pits was argued over, because the 1986 samples were too poor for reliable radiocarbon dating. In 2022, after Peking University's laboratory dated nearly 200 samples, mostly burned bamboo, the provincial institute announced that Pits 3, 4, 7 and 8 were buried between about 1131 and 1012 BCE, in the late Shang period. Pits 5 and 6 may be a little different in age. The excavators' Antiquity report places them in the early Western Zhou on the style of their contents, while a 2023 radiocarbon range for Pit 6 overlaps the others, so that point is still open.

## Isolated, but not cut off

Sanxingdui was once called an isolated civilization, but the evidence points the other way. Its bronze vessels borrow Shang forms, and the decoration on some figures resembles bronzes from the middle Yangtze. Bronzes at Sanxingdui and at Anyang share an unusual "highly radiogenic" lead isotope signature, which suggests a shared, still unidentified metal source. Geochemists disagree about where that ore came from. Proposals include Yunnan and the Sichuan–Yunnan border, and a 2023 study even argued that Sanxingdui was a hub of the metal trade. Others point out that there is no clear Bronze Age mining evidence in Yunnan this early.

## Why bury the gods?

Why would a city smash, burn and bury its most precious images? The earliest idea was a sudden attack. That is now less popular, because the pits were dug in a tidy row, which suggests a planned ritual. Archaeologist Lei Yu has suggested that after a flood or earthquake, people may have decided their gods had failed them. Historian Sun Hua has argued for a power struggle between priestly and secular elites, perhaps tied to the Zhou conquest of the Shang. These are hypotheses, not findings.

What is clear is that around 1000 BCE the centre of Shu shifted to **Jinsha**, in today's Chengdu, where similar gold and bronze objects continue. As for the claims online that Sanxingdui's faces are evidence of aliens, no archaeologist working on the site takes them seriously. The faces are the work of skilled human casters, whose language and beliefs we still cannot read.
