## A city nobody remembered building

On the floodplain of the Mississippi River in southwestern Illinois, opposite St. Louis, stands the largest prehistoric earthwork in the Americas north of Mexico. Around it lie the remains of **Cahokia**, a city that flourished from roughly 1050 to 1350 CE. UNESCO, which listed it as a World Heritage Site in 1982, calls it the largest pre-Columbian settlement north of Mexico, once spread over about 1,600 hectares with some 120 mounds. About 70 survive inside today's state historic site.

We do not know what its people called it. "Cahokia" comes from a subgroup of the Illinois peoples who lived nearby in the 1700s, long after the city emptied. Its builders belonged to the **Mississippian** culture, farming societies that raised platform mounds across the Mississippi valley and the Southeast; Cahokia was the earliest and largest of their centres.

## Monks Mound and the Grand Plaza

The heart of the city was **Monks Mound**, about 30 metres high and covering roughly 6 hectares at its base, built in stages between about 900 and 1200 CE. Britannica estimates that it holds more than 700,000 cubic metres of earth. On its summit stood a huge building, probably the seat of the ruling elite. The name is recent: French Trappist monks lived on a smaller mound nearby in the early 1800s and gardened the big mound's first terrace.

South of the mound lay the **Grand Plaza**, nearly 16 hectares that were deliberately filled and levelled. Around the core, neighbourhoods, smaller plazas and outlying "suburbs" were laid out on the same orientation to the cardinal directions. The archaeologist Timothy Pauketat calls the sudden rebuilding of the town around 1050 Cahokia's **"Big Bang"**: older villages were cleared and a planned city replaced them within a generation or two.

How many people lived there is one of the big open questions. UNESCO gives 10,000 to 20,000 at the peak, between about 1050 and 1150, while published estimates have ranged from about 8,000 to as many as 40,000. A 2014 study of strontium isotopes in tooth enamel found that about **one-third** of the individuals tested had grown up somewhere else, suggesting the city grew partly by drawing in newcomers from across the region.

## Sun circles and the Birdman

In the 1960s the archaeologist Warren Wittry found rings of large post pits west of Monks Mound. These **"woodhenges"**, circles of red cedar posts rebuilt several times, appear to mark where the sun rose at the solstices and equinoxes.

![A tall wooden post standing in a grassy field, part of a reconstructed ring of posts](images/cahokia-woodhenge.jpg)
*Part of the reconstructed woodhenge at Cahokia, with the central post placed as it would be for the equinoxes. Photo: QuartierLatin1968, [CC BY-SA 3.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/3.0/), via [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Woodhenge_Cahokia_3998.jpg).*

Cahokia's religious imagery survives in carved stone, copper and shell. Its best-known emblem is the **Birdman Tablet**, a small sandstone piece about 10 centimetres tall found in 1971 on the east side of Monks Mound. It shows a dancing human figure with a beaked mask and a feathered wing. Some scholars link it to Morning Star, a hero of later Native stories, but that reading relies on traditions recorded centuries afterwards.

![A small engraved stone tablet showing a figure in a bird mask with a wing, displayed on a stand in a museum case](images/cahokia-birdman-tablet.jpg)

## Mound 72

Between 1967 and 1971 Melvin Fowler excavated a small ridgetop mound called **Mound 72** and found about 270 bodies buried between roughly 1000 and 1200 CE. They included mass graves of mostly young people, many of whom seem to have been sacrificed. At the centre lay the famous "beaded burial": one person laid on a bed of about 20,000 shell beads, with another beneath. For decades both were described as men, and the scene was read as a monument to a male warrior-chief.

A reanalysis led by Thomas Emerson and published in 2016 changed that picture. It found that the central pair were a man and a woman, that twelve people rather than six belonged to the burial, and that men and women were paired throughout. The authors now read the mound as a ritual about creation, renewal and fertility rather than one man's power. Dental studies have also found men in mass graves once thought to hold only women.

## Why did Cahokia empty out?

From about 1175 to 1275 the people of Cahokia built a palisade about three kilometres long around the central precinct and rebuilt it four times, each time needing an estimated 20,000 logs. They clearly felt threatened, though no one knows by whom. By about 1350 the city and much of the surrounding region were largely abandoned.

The causes are still debated:

- **Deforestation and local flooding.** A theory first put forward in 1993 held that clearing the uplands for wood caused erosion and floods in Cahokia's fields. A 2021 study led by Caitlin Rankin found that the ground surface under one mound stayed stable from Mississippian times until the 1800s, which counts strongly against it.
- **Mississippi River floods and drought.** Sediment cores studied by Samuel Muñoz and colleagues in 2015 suggest that Cahokia rose during centuries with few big floods and declined after they returned after about 1200. A 2019 study of ancient human waste in lake mud (fecal stanols) linked the start of the population decline to a major flood and drier summers around 1150.
- **Politics and society.** Many archaeologists stress that conflict, factional strife and people choosing to leave mattered as much as the environment, and that several causes probably combined.

![Timeline of Cahokia from about 650 to 1450 CE](images/cahokia-timeline.svg)

Cahokia's people did not vanish. They dispersed and joined other communities. The Osage Nation, whose oral traditions place the Dhegiha Siouan peoples in the Cahokia and St. Louis area before they moved west, counts the mound builders among its ancestors.
