## The land between the rivers

Sumer lay in the far south of Mesopotamia, in what is now southern Iraq, where the Tigris and Euphrates approach the Persian Gulf. The land was flat, hot and dry, but the rivers carried rich silt. From roughly 5000 BCE, farmers there learned to dig canals and channel floodwater onto their fields. Irrigation produced grain surpluses, and surpluses supported larger settlements, specialists and temples.

## Uruk and the urban revolution

By about 3500 to 3100 BCE, Uruk had grown into one of the largest settlements in the world, with perhaps tens of thousands of inhabitants and monumental temple complexes such as the Eanna precinct. Other city-states followed, including Ur, Lagash, Kish, Nippur and Eridu. Each city was devoted to a patron god, and its temple was both a religious centre and an economic one that managed land, labour and stored goods.

## The invention of writing

Sumer's most famous contribution is writing. Around 3300 to 3200 BCE, administrators at Uruk began pressing signs into clay tablets to record rations, livestock and workers. These early signs were largely pictures, but over the following centuries they became the wedge-shaped marks we call **cuneiform**. Cuneiform eventually recorded laws, letters, hymns, mathematics and literature, and it was adapted for many other languages, including Akkadian, Babylonian and Hittite.

## Kings, law and literature

Sumerian city-states often fought over water and land. Rulers called *en*, *ensi* or *lugal* claimed to govern on behalf of the gods. Around 2100 BCE, Ur-Nammu of Ur issued one of the oldest known law codes, centuries before Hammurabi. Sumerian literature includes the poems about Gilgamesh, a legendary king of Uruk whose stories were later woven into the Babylonian *Epic of Gilgamesh*.

## Legacy

Sumer was absorbed by Akkad under Sargon around 2300 BCE, revived under the Third Dynasty of Ur, and faded as a spoken language by about 2000 BCE. Yet scribes kept studying Sumerian for nearly two thousand years. Key legacies include:

- Cuneiform writing and the scribal school
- A base-60 number system, still echoed in 60-minute hours and 360-degree circles
- Ziggurats, city planning and large-scale irrigation
- Literary themes, such as a great flood, that appear in later traditions
