## Rediscovering a lost power

For centuries the Hittites were known mainly as a name in the Hebrew Bible, where "Hittites" appear among the peoples of Canaan. In the nineteenth century, scholars began to notice references in Egyptian and Assyrian records to a powerful kingdom called **Hatti**, and travellers reported strange hieroglyphic inscriptions in Anatolia and northern Syria. The breakthrough came in 1906, when excavations led by Hugo Winckler at Boğazköy (modern Boğazkale) in central Turkey uncovered thousands of clay tablets. The site proved to be **Hattusa**, the Hittite capital.

In 1915 the Czech scholar Bedřich Hrozný argued that the main language of the tablets was Indo-European, related to Greek, Latin and Sanskrit. His decipherment was controversial at first but is now universally accepted. Hittite is the oldest well-documented Indo-European language, which makes the archive important for linguists as well as historians. Modern scholars note one irony: the Hittites called their language *nešili*, "the language of Nesa," and the name "Hittite" is borrowed from the earlier, non-Indo-European Hattian people whose land they took over.

## Rise of the kingdom

The Hittite state emerged in central Anatolia in roughly the seventeenth century BCE. Tradition credits an early king, Hattusili I, with making Hattusa his capital, and his successor Mursili I with a famous long-distance raid that sacked Babylon around 1595 BCE (some scholars prefer a slightly different date, because Bronze Age chronology is still debated). The kingdom then went through a period of court murders and instability. A king named Telipinu issued an edict, often dated to about the late sixteenth century BCE, that tried to fix rules of royal succession.

Historians conventionally divide Hittite history into an Old Kingdom and a later New Kingdom or "Empire" period, sometimes with a debated Middle Kingdom in between. The boundaries of these phases are argued over, and absolute dates usually carry a margin of decades.

## The empire and Kadesh

The Hittites reached their height in the fourteenth and thirteenth centuries BCE. King **Suppiluliuma I** (reigning roughly 1350–1322 BCE) defeated the kingdom of Mitanni and extended Hittite control over much of northern Syria, installing relatives as vassal kings at Carchemish and Aleppo. This brought the Hittites face to face with Egypt.

The rivalry culminated in the **Battle of Kadesh** in Syria, around 1274 BCE, between the Hittite king Muwatalli II and Ramesses II of Egypt. Ramesses celebrated it on temple walls as a great victory, but most historians judge it a stalemate at best, and the Hittites kept control of the region. About sixteen years later, Hattusili III and Ramesses II concluded a peace treaty. Versions survive in both Egyptian hieroglyphs and Akkadian cuneiform, and it is often cited as one of the earliest surviving peace treaties in the world. Hattusili's queen, **Puduhepa**, corresponded with the Egyptian court and played an unusually visible role in diplomacy.

## Religion, law and daily life

The Hittites called their land the realm of "the thousand gods." Rather than replacing local cults, they absorbed deities from Hattian, Hurrian, Luwian and Mesopotamian traditions. The storm god and the sun goddess of Arinna stood at the head of the pantheon, and the king acted as chief priest. The open-air rock sanctuary of **Yazılıkaya**, near Hattusa, shows processions of carved gods and goddesses, probably from the thirteenth century BCE.

A collection of Hittite laws survives in copies from different periods. Compared with some other ancient codes, it relied heavily on compensation and fines rather than physical punishment, and later versions record reductions of earlier penalties. Historians caution, however, that law collections show ideals and scribal traditions, and may not reflect everyday court practice. The tablets also include treaties, prayers, rituals, oracle reports and letters, which together offer a rich picture of how the state worked.

## Collapse and legacy

Around 1200 BCE, or within a few decades after, Hattusa was abandoned and partly burned, and the Hittite empire ceased to exist. Its fall belongs to the wider **Late Bronze Age collapse** that also struck Mycenaean Greece, Ugarit and other states. Explanations include drought and famine, internal rebellion, disrupted trade, and the movements of the so-called Sea Peoples. Recent excavations suggest the capital may have been largely emptied before any destruction, and most scholars now favour a combination of causes rather than a single invasion.

Hittite culture did not vanish completely. In southeast Anatolia and northern Syria, small "Neo-Hittite" or Syro-Hittite kingdoms, such as Carchemish, continued for several centuries and used Luwian hieroglyphic inscriptions. These states are probably the "Hittites" known to the biblical writers, until Assyria absorbed them in the eighth century BCE. Today Hattusa is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and its archives remain one of the richest sources for the Bronze Age world.
