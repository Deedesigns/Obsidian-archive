## A sanctuary on Parnassus

Delphi sits on a steep shelf beneath the cliffs of Mount Parnassus, looking down toward the Gulf of Corinth. The Greeks called it the *omphalos*, the navel of the world: in myth, Zeus released two eagles from opposite ends of the earth, and they met here. People lived on the spot in the Mycenaean age, roughly 1500 to 1100 BCE, but according to UNESCO, which listed the site in 1987, the sanctuary and its oracle only began to develop in the 8th century BCE. By the 6th century BCE Delphi had become the religious centre of the Greek world.

The god who spoke there was Apollo. The Homeric *Hymn to Apollo*, probably composed in the early 6th century BCE, tells how he killed a monstrous serpent at the site and chose Cretan sailors as his first priests. Later writers added rival origin stories: Aeschylus says the oracle once belonged to Earth and then to Themis before passing to Apollo, and Diodorus tells of a goatherd whose animals fell into a trance beside a crack in the ground. These are myths, not history, but they show that the Greeks themselves believed the shrine was older than Apollo.

## The woman on the tripod

The voice of Apollo was a woman called the **Pythia**. She was a Delphian, and according to Diodorus the post was once held by young virgins until one was abducted; after that the Pythia was a woman over fifty who dressed as a maiden. Plutarch, who served as a priest at Delphi around the end of the 1st century CE and wrote several essays about the oracle, says that at the shrine's busiest the sanctuary kept two Pythias taking turns, with a third in reserve.

Consultations were rationed. Late sources say the oracle originally spoke once a year, on Apollo's birthday, the seventh day of the month Bysios, and later on the seventh of each month during the nine warmer months; in winter Apollo was said to be away. After purification at the Castalian spring and the sacrifice of a goat, the Pythia went down into the *adyton*, the restricted inner room of the temple, and sat on a bronze tripod. Inquirers paid a fee, and cities and favoured visitors could jump the queue. On the temple porch were carved maxims such as "Know thyself" and "Nothing in excess," which Pausanias saw still in place in the 2nd century CE.

## Riddles, or plain advice?

Delphi's most famous answers are the ambiguous ones. Herodotus says that when Croesus of Lydia asked whether to attack Persia, he was told "that if he should send an army against the Persians he would destroy a great empire." He did, and the empire he destroyed was his own. In 480 BCE, as Xerxes advanced, the Athenians received a terrifying first response and then a second promising that "a wood-built wall" would protect them and that "Divine Salamis" would "bring death to women's sons." Themistocles argued that the wooden wall meant the fleet, and the Greeks won at Salamis.

Many historians doubt that these stories record real responses word for word. In a landmark 1978 study, Joseph Fontenrose sorted every known Delphic response by how reliably it is attested and concluded that the well-documented ones are mostly short, clear instructions on religious matters, while the dramatic riddling prophecies belong largely to legend and literature. Herodotus wrote decades after the events, and an oracle remembered after the fact can be polished to fit what happened.

The other big argument is about who spoke. A popular modern picture has the Pythia raving in a trance while male priests turned her cries into verse. Fontenrose and the classicist Lisa Maurizio argue that the ancient sources consistently show the Pythia speaking intelligibly in her own voice, sometimes even in verse. Most specialists now treat the "raving priestess and clever priests" model as unproven.

## Was there really a vapour?

Strabo and Plutarch both mention a *pneuma*, a breath or vapour, rising in the adyton, and Plutarch describes the room occasionally filling with a fragrance "as if" from "the sweetest and most expensive perfumes." When French archaeologists excavated the temple from 1892, they reported no chasm, and through most of the 20th century scholars such as Adolphe Paul Oppé, E. R. Dodds and Pierre Amandry treated the vapour as a myth.

In 2001 the geologist Jelle Zeilinga de Boer, the archaeologist John Hale and the chemist Jeffrey Chanton argued in the journal *Geology* that two faults cross beneath the temple and that springs nearby carried light hydrocarbons; they proposed that sweet-smelling ethylene gas could have induced a mild trance. The idea drew wide attention and sharp criticism. In 2007 Jay Foster and Daryn Lehoux argued in *Clinical Toxicology* that the ethylene concentrations needed were implausible, and other geologists have proposed different gases or doubted that any gas was strong enough to matter. The faults are real; whether a gas shaped the Pythia's experience remains an open and lively debate.

## Gold, war and silence

Whatever happened in the adyton, Delphi's influence was enormous. Cities consulted it before founding colonies and passing religious laws, and they filled the Sacred Way with treasuries and statues. The sanctuary hosted the Pythian Games, and its wealth made it a prize: UNESCO counts four "sacred wars" fought over control of it. The temple itself burned in 548 BCE and was rebuilt with help from the exiled Athenian Alcmaeonid family; after an earthquake in 373 BCE it was rebuilt again, and those 4th-century foundations are what visitors see today.

Under Rome the oracle continued, though Plutarch already wrote an essay on why oracles were in decline. It seems to have fallen silent in the late 4th century CE, when the emperor Theodosius I banned pagan sacrifice. A famous "last oracle," supposedly given to the emperor Julian and lamenting that Apollo "no longer has a hut," survives only in later Christian sources, and its authenticity is doubtful.
