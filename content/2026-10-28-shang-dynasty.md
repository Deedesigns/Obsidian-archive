## Dragon bones in a medicine shop

In late 19th-century China, farmers near Anyang in Henan province kept turning up old bones in their fields. Pharmacies bought them as "dragon bones," to be ground into powder for traditional medicine. The story goes that in 1899 the scholar **Wang Yirong** was looking over the dragon bones in his own prescription, before they were pounded into powder, when he noticed carved signs and recognised them as an archaic form of Chinese writing. The anecdote may be polished in the retelling, but the discovery was real.

Dealers kept their source secret. As the British Library's curators explain, it was only traced in 1914 to **Yinxu**, "the Ruins of Yin," near Anyang, and scientific excavation began in 1928. What the diggers found was the last capital of the **Shang**, the earliest Chinese dynasty confirmed by its own written records.

## A dynasty between texts and trowels

Later Chinese histories, above all Sima Qian's *Records of the Grand Historian* from about 100 BCE, describe a Shang dynasty founded by King Tang, which ruled the central plain before the Zhou overthrew it. Before the oracle bones, many historians had no way to test that account. In 1917 the scholar **Wang Guowei** matched royal names in the oracle-bone sacrifices against Sima Qian's king list and found that it was basically right, with a few corrections. A list written down roughly a thousand years after the fact had preserved real kings.

How far back the Shang go is harder. China's state-sponsored Xia–Shang–Zhou Chronology Project, which reported in 2000, put the start of the dynasty at about 1600 BCE. Huge rammed-earth walls at **Zhengzhou**, nearly 7 kilometres around, are usually treated as an early Shang centre from the so-called Erligang period, though which historical capital they represent is still argued.

Earlier still is the **Erlitou** culture near Luoyang. Most Chinese archaeologists identify it with the **Xia**, the dynasty that tradition places before the Shang. Many scholars outside China prefer to call it simply "the Erlitou state," because no writing from the site names it. This is one of the liveliest debates in Chinese archaeology, and neither side has yet produced decisive evidence.

## Asking the ancestors

According to UNESCO, King **Pan Geng** moved the court to Yin around 1300 BCE, and the city stayed the capital for about 255 years under twelve kings. From the reign of **Wu Ding**, in roughly the 13th or 12th century BCE (exact reign dates differ between chronologies), the kings left thousands of divination records.

The method is well understood. Diviners drilled hollows into polished turtle undershells (plastrons) or cattle shoulder blades, then pressed a hot rod to them until the bone cracked with a sharp sound. A question was often put both ways, for example "Fu Hao's childbirth will be fortunate" and "will not be fortunate," and the king read the cracks. Afterwards a scribe might carve the date, the diviner's name, the question and sometimes the result into the bone. The topics range from harvests, weather and war to dreams, illness and the king's toothache. Most of these questions went to royal ancestors and to a high god called **Di**.

The script was already a mature system, the direct ancestor of modern Chinese characters. Even the record has a catch, though. The British Library notes that about 90 percent of oracle bones carry no writing at all, and fakes began appearing almost as soon as collectors started paying by the character.

## Fu Hao, queen and general

One name recurs in Wu Ding's divinations: **Fu Hao**, one of his consorts. The inscriptions show her leading troops against enemy peoples, presiding over sacrifices and controlling her own estate. In 1976 the archaeologist **Zheng Zhenxiang** and her team found a modest pit tomb, about 5.6 by 4 metres, just outside the royal cemetery. Unlike the great royal tombs, which were robbed in antiquity, it was untouched. Bronzes inside bore Fu Hao's name.

The contents were staggering: 468 bronze objects, including some 130 weapons, 755 jades, 564 objects of bone and about 6,900 cowrie shells. Some jades were already ancient when she was buried, made by Neolithic cultures centuries earlier, which suggests she collected antiques. UNESCO calls it the only intact tomb of a Shang royal family member yet found.

## Bronze on an industrial scale

Shang craftsmen did not hammer their bronzes. They cast them in sectioned clay moulds, and excavations at foundry sites such as Xiaomintun in Anyang have turned up thousands of mould fragments. The most famous result is the **Houmuwu ding**, a square cauldron found near Anyang in the 1930s and dedicated to a king's mother. The National Museum of China gives its weight as 832.84 kilograms, making it the heaviest known ancient Chinese bronze. Its body and legs were cast in one pour, which the museum estimates needed more than a tonne of metal, and the handles were added afterwards.

## The darker side of the ritual

The same rites that produced these vessels also consumed people. Oracle-bone inscriptions mention at least 14,197 human victims, according to a count by the historian Hu Houxuan cited in later scholarship, and many were **Qiang**, a people from the west. Scholars have estimated that more than 13,000 people were sacrificed at Yinxu over about two centuries, most of them young men. In 2017 a team led by bioarchaeologist **Christina Cheung** studied isotopes in the bones of 68 victims from the royal cemetery. Their diets suggested they came from outside Yinxu but had lived there for a few years before death, which the team argued means captives were probably put to work before they were killed.

## The fall

According to later tradition, the last Shang king, remembered by the Zhou as a tyrant, was defeated by King Wu of Zhou at the battle of **Muye**. The Chronology Project dated the conquest to 1046 BCE, but that date is contested; other scholars, working from the *Bamboo Annals* and astronomical records, argue for about 1045 or 1040 BCE. The victors wrote the story of the Shang's moral decline, so it's best read as Zhou propaganda as much as history. Yinxu itself fell silent, and its bones waited three thousand years for a sick scholar to read them.
