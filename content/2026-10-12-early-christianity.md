## A Jewish beginning

Christianity began in Roman-ruled Judea and Galilee in the early first century CE. Jesus of Nazareth, a Jewish teacher and healer, preached about the coming kingdom of God. Most historians agree that he was baptised by John the Baptist, gathered disciples, and was crucified in Jerusalem under the Roman governor Pontius Pilate, around 30 to 33 CE. His followers proclaimed that he had risen from the dead and was the Messiah (in Greek, *Christos*).

## From Jerusalem outward

The earliest community, led by figures such as Peter and James, the brother of Jesus, remained within Judaism. A key early debate was whether non-Jewish converts had to follow Jewish law, including circumcision. **Paul of Tarsus**, a former persecutor of the movement, became its most influential missionary to Gentiles, founding communities in cities such as Corinth, Philippi and Thessalonica. His letters, written in the 50s CE, are the oldest surviving Christian texts.

## Texts and identity

The Gospels of Mark, Matthew, Luke and John were probably written between about 65 and 100 CE. After the Romans destroyed the Jerusalem Temple in 70 CE, Christianity and rabbinic Judaism gradually developed into distinct traditions. Christians met in homes, shared a ritual meal (the Eucharist), and developed leadership roles such as bishops, elders and deacons.

## Persecution and growth

Christians were a minority, sometimes viewed with suspicion for refusing to honour Roman gods. Persecution was sporadic and local at first, as under Nero in 64 CE, and became empire-wide under emperors such as Decius (250 CE) and Diocletian (from 303 CE). Even so, the faith grew steadily through networks of family, trade and charity.

## Toward an imperial church

Major turning points came in the fourth century:

- 313 CE: the Edict of Milan, under Constantine and Licinius, granted religious toleration
- 325 CE: the Council of Nicaea addressed debates about Christ's divinity and produced the Nicene Creed
- 380 CE: under Theodosius I, Nicene Christianity became the official religion of the Roman Empire
